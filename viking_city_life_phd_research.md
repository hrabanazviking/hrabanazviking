# The Urban North
## Economy, Work, Household, Property, Governance, Mobility, and Everyday Life in Viking Cities, c. 700–1100 CE

**A doctoral-scale synthetic research paper and production reference**  
**Prepared for Volmarr**  
**Research synthesis current to 17 October 2026**

---

## Abstract

Viking cities were not simply large villages and they were not miniature versions of later medieval municipalities. They were a historically distinctive urban form: compact, unusually regulated concentrations of people whose livelihoods depended heavily on craft, commerce, transport, administration, and services rather than direct food production; nodal harbours that connected agrarian hinterlands to maritime and riverine exchange; political instruments through which rulers could protect, tax, supervise, and profit from commerce; and social laboratories in which immigrants, local households, itinerant merchants, sailors, craft specialists, warriors, dependants, clerics, enslaved people, women, and children lived at much greater density than was normal in the Viking countryside. Archaeology now permits a much more intimate reconstruction of these communities than older narratives centered on “trade routes” alone.

This paper synthesizes archaeological excavation, settlement morphology, numismatics, zooarchaeology, archaeobotany, environmental archaeology, isotopic mobility studies, ancient DNA, craft studies, burial archaeology, historical texts, legal comparison, and recent theories of early Scandinavian urbanism. Its principal case studies are **Ribe, Hedeby, Birka, Kaupang, Jorvik/York, and Dublin**, with comparative evidence from **Sigtuna, Truso, Wolin, and Staraya Ladoga**. Particular attention is paid to questions often left unanswered in general Viking histories: What did a household actually do all day? How was an urban plot organized? How did food reach a population that could not feed itself from its own fields? What kinds of work existed? Were “merchant,” “craftsperson,” and “householder” separate occupations? What did people use as money? Who maintained streets and harbours? How did rulers intervene in commerce? Could foreigners settle? How did someone acquire a house or plot? Who owned the ground? How did women, children, servants, and enslaved persons fit into the urban economy? What were sanitation, crowding, health, noise, smoke, smell, and social life like?

The central argument is that the Viking city functioned through the interaction of five systems: **(1) a household-plot economy**, in which residence, craft, storage, exchange, and family life overlapped; **(2) a hinterland provisioning economy**, which supplied food, fuel, animals, timber, fibre, and other necessities; **(3) a long-distance network economy**, moving portable high-value goods, raw materials, silver, people, and information; **(4) a political-juridical framework**, often fostered by rulers who protected and taxed commerce and probably controlled or authorized aspects of urban tenure; and **(5) an intensely mobile social population**, in which permanent residents coexisted with seasonal traders, sailors, visitors, officials, foreigners, captives, and migrants. The city was therefore less a self-contained organism than a controlled membrane between local production and the wider Viking world.

The evidence also establishes important limits. No surviving Viking-Age archive gives us a complete deed, lease, rent roll, zoning ordinance, or occupational census for Ribe, Hedeby, Birka, or Kaupang. Plot regularity can demonstrate planning and durable tenure without proving modern freehold property. Later town laws can illuminate possibilities but cannot simply be projected backward. Likewise, an artefact assemblage can identify metalworking or textile production without proving that every person who performed it was a full-time professional. Where the evidence is inferential rather than direct, this paper labels it accordingly.

# 1. Scope, terminology, and thesis

### 1.1 What this paper means by “Viking city”

This paper deliberately uses **Viking city** and **Viking town** as readable umbrella terms for permanent or substantially permanent nucleated settlements of the Viking world whose population, spatial organization, and economy were significantly more urban than the surrounding agrarian landscape. Archaeologists often distinguish *emporia*, ports of trade, early towns, proto-towns, central places, landing places, seasonal markets, and later administrative or ecclesiastical towns. Those distinctions remain analytically valuable. They do not require us to avoid ordinary language. Ribe, Hedeby, Birka, Kaupang, Jorvik, and Dublin were different kinds of urban communities at different moments, but all can legitimately be studied as parts of Viking urban life (Skre 2007; Corsi 2020; Kalmring 2024; Richards 2026).

The chronological core is approximately **700–1100 CE**. This captures the formation of Ribe in the early eighth century, the rise of Birka, Hedeby, Kaupang, and other emporia, the explosive development of Scandinavian urban power in York and Dublin, and the transition toward the more recognizably medieval towns of the tenth and eleventh centuries. The terminal date is intentionally porous because several urban processes that began in the Viking Age become clearest in the eleventh or early twelfth century. Sigtuna, for example, is indispensable for studying immigration and later Viking urbanization even though much of its best biological evidence straddles the conventional Viking/medieval boundary (Krzewińska et al. 2018).

### 1.2 The central thesis

The Viking city should be understood as a **regulated exchange ecology**. It worked because several normally dispersed activities were compressed into a small area: craft production, storage, ship traffic, weighing and payment, consumption, legal supervision, political representation, household life, and contact with strangers. This concentration reduced the friction of exchange. A ship arriving with glass, wine, silver, stone, antler, or imported pottery did not need to negotiate separately with a hundred farms. A craftsperson could obtain raw materials, find buyers, exchange silver, and learn new techniques in the same settlement. A ruler could protect merchants, supervise a harbour, collect customs, and monitor strategically important foreigners without trying to police an entire coastline. A rural producer could bring surplus cattle, hides, grain, wool, fish, fuel, or timber to a place where demand was dense and recurrent (Sindbæk 2007a; Skre 2007; Kalmring 2024; Sindbæk 2023).

The city was therefore not economically independent. Quite the opposite: urban specialization **increased dependence** on the countryside. Dagfinn Skre has emphasized that a non-food-producing population must engage in repeated everyday transactions simply to survive. Recent isotopic evidence from Birka makes the point unusually concrete: cattle could be brought from more than 180 km away, while other animals were supplied more locally, demonstrating a layered provisioning system rather than a self-sufficient urban herd (Skre 2008; Schjerven et al. 2024).

### 1.3 Five systems made the city function

For analytical purposes, this paper treats Viking urbanism as the coupling of five systems:

1. **The household-plot system.** A household occupied a bounded urban space in which dwelling, craft, storage, cooking, exchange, and family life could coexist. The modern distinction between residence and workplace is frequently misleading (Boyd 2013; Reilly 2024; Sindbæk ed. 2022).
2. **The hinterland system.** Farms, fisheries, forests, quarry zones, herds, and rural craft production supplied calories, fuel, timber, fibre, animals, stone, iron, and other bulky necessities (Schjerven et al. 2024; Corsi 2020).
3. **The long-distance network system.** Ships and river craft moved small high-value goods, currencies, raw materials, technologies, people, news, and cultural practices over the North Sea, Baltic, Atlantic, and eastern river systems (Sindbæk 2007a, 2007b, 2023; Ashby and Sindbæk eds. 2020).
4. **The political-juridical system.** Rulers and their representatives provided or claimed protection, jurisdiction, defence, market supervision, customs revenue, and at least some authority over urban space. Kalmring’s recent synthesis interprets Scandinavian emporia as special economic and legal zones, although the exact legal rules are incompletely preserved (Kalmring 2024, esp. chs. 7–9).
5. **The mobility system.** Urban growth depended on movement. Isotope and ancient-DNA studies show mixtures of local, regional, and long-distance origins, while artefacts and written sources reveal travelling merchants, ship crews, craftspeople, clerics, warriors, captives, and diplomats (Croix et al. 2020; Krzewińska et al. 2018; Rimbert, *Vita Anskarii*).

None of these systems alone created a city. Their **interaction** did.

# 2. Evidence and method

## 2.1 Why Viking urban history is unusually archaeological

The Viking world did not leave municipal archives comparable to those of later medieval towns. We do not possess an eighth-century Ribe rent roll, a ninth-century Kaupang street register, a Hedeby book of customs accounts, or a Birka census. Written sources are sparse, external, late, tendentious, or episodic. That absence explains why Viking urban history must be built from material evidence at a much finer scale than many later periods (Kalmring 2024, 84–104).

Urban archaeology compensates magnificently. Waterlogged deposits at York and Dublin preserve wood, leather, textiles, insects, seeds, floor layers, fences, tools, and organic rubbish. Ribe’s high-resolution stratigraphy allows activity to be traced across houses and workshops. Kaupang’s excavated plots preserve chronological changes in occupation and exchange. Hedeby combines settlement archaeology, harbour archaeology, geophysics, fortification, graves, and ship remains. Birka combines the “Black Earth” settlement, extensive cemeteries, fortifications, artefact networks, and new biomolecular research (Skre ed. 2007; Ambrosiani 2013, 2021; Wallace 2016; Sindbæk ed. 2022; Reilly 2024).

## 2.2 Major evidence classes

**Settlement morphology** tells us about plots, streets, paths, houses, rebuilding, waterfronts, access, boundaries, and density. Repeated plot lines are especially important because they reveal durable social claims to space even when we do not know the legal term for those claims (Pilø 2007; Simpson 1998; Reilly 2024; Kalmring 2024).

**Artefact distribution** identifies craft zones and household activities: crucibles, moulds, slag, hammerscale, spindle whorls, loom weights, needles, comb blanks, antler waste, glass cullet, bead fragments, leather offcuts, whetstones, pottery, scales, weights, silver fragments, imported vessels, gaming pieces, musical instruments, keys, locks, and personal ornaments. Context matters more than the mere presence of an object (Ashby and Sindbæk eds. 2020; Sindbæk eds. 2022, 2023).

**Environmental archaeology** reconstructs what written texts normally ignore: animal species, butchery, fish, plant foods, fuel, pests, dung, wetness, waste, floor conditions, local vegetation, and the ecology of crowded settlement. Insect remains from Dublin and York are particularly powerful because different beetle and fly communities indicate rotting organic material, stable waste, stored products, damp floor material, and other microenvironments (Addyman 1989; Reilly 2024).

**Zooarchaeology and isotopes** turn food into geography. Animal bones tell us what was eaten, skinned, processed, and discarded; isotopic signatures can show whether animals grew up near the town or were driven in from farther away. Birka’s 2024 multi-isotope study is a model for treating livestock as “walking commodities” (Schjerven et al. 2024).

**Human isotope and genetic evidence** addresses migration directly. At early Ribe, strontium measurements on 21 individuals indicate that most values fell within a relatively narrow local/regional range while a minority were non-local; cultural affinities could extend beyond geographic birthplace. At Sigtuna, 23 genomes and 31 strontium datasets indicate high diversity and a mixture of local, regional, and long-distance origins, with substantial mobility among both males and females (Croix et al. 2020; Krzewińska et al. 2018).

**Numismatics and metrology** reveal transactional behaviour. Coins, hacksilver, ingots, weights, and balances show when and where exchange became monetized, how imported silver was converted into local payment systems, and how standardized weights facilitated dealings among strangers (Skre ed. 2008; Graham-Campbell ed. 2018).

**Burial archaeology** provides imperfect but important evidence for identity, status, mobility, religion, dress, gendered material practice, weaponry, and social differentiation. Birka’s thousands of graves demonstrate a socially varied urban community; richly equipped graves include women and children as well as men (Price et al. 2019; Swedish History Museum 2025/2026).

**Texts** provide names and institutions rarely visible in dirt. Rimbert’s ninth-century *Vita Anskarii*, for example, places at Birka a king, a local prefect or official, merchants, townspeople, and collective deliberation. Ohthere’s account identifies Skiringssal/Kaupang as a maritime stop on a repeated long-distance route. Annals and chronicles illuminate Dublin and York as political centres, while Arabic authors show the eastern and Baltic world of river-borne Viking exchange. Each text must be handled according to genre and agenda rather than mined as neutral reportage (Rimbert; *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle*; *Annals of Ulster*; Ibn Fadlan).

## 2.3 An explicit evidence hierarchy

This paper uses four confidence grades:

- **Grade A: Direct and repeated evidence.** Excavated features, independently replicated scientific results, securely identified production waste, contemporary textual testimony supported by archaeology.
- **Grade B: Strong inference.** A pattern best explained by a particular institution or behaviour, but not named directly by a contemporary source.
- **Grade C: Plausible reconstruction.** Consistent with evidence and close comparisons but not demonstrable for every town or date.
- **Grade D: Speculation / later analogy.** Potentially useful for hypothesis, but should never be presented as established Viking-Age fact.

This matters most in discussions of **property, occupational specialization, municipal law, rents, taverns, policing, guilds, and urban citizenship**, all topics on which later medieval institutions can seduce historians into reading backwards.

# 3. What made a Viking city urban?

## 3.1 Urbanity was a bundle, not a population threshold

No single demographic cutoff separates a Viking town from a large settlement. Kaupang may have contained only several hundred people; Birka has often been estimated at roughly 700–1,000; yet both were qualitatively different from ordinary rural settlements because of compactness, non-agrarian activity, long-distance connections, plots, harbour orientation, specialized production, and repeated dealings among unrelated people (Skre 2007; Price et al. 2019; Kalmring 2024).

The strongest recurring urban signals are:

- a **dense, bounded or clearly delimited settlement**;
- **plots or tenements** arranged along streets, paths, waterfronts, or market axes;
- a high concentration of **craft production**;
- **permanent or recurrent habitation** beyond a brief fair;
- participation in **regional and long-distance exchange**;
- a population substantially dependent on food supplied from elsewhere;
- **transactional infrastructure**, including weights, scales, coins, silver, storage, landing places, and organized access;
- some form of **political protection, jurisdiction, or regulation**;
- marked **mobility and social diversity**;
- over time, fortification, royal administration, minting, churches, or other institutional architecture (Skre 2007; Corsi 2020; Richards 2026; Kalmring 2024).

## 3.2 From seasonal market to permanent neighbourhood

The earliest phases of some towns were more seasonal than later phases. The crucial change was not simply that trade increased, but that **people began investing in durable place**. Permanent houses, rebuilt plot boundaries, workshops operating over generations, streets maintained through repeated occupation, wells, fences, and local cemeteries all signal a resident community with memory and claims (Croix 2015; Skre 2007; Sindbæk ed. 2022).

Ribe is especially important because older interpretations treated early emporia as largely seasonal fairs. Sarah Croix’s reassessment argues that at least part of early Ribe was permanently inhabited from the first decades of the eighth century. Northern Emporium excavations subsequently revealed dense stratified sequences of dwellings, workshops, plots, and craft activity that make a purely seasonal model untenable for the settlement as a whole (Croix 2015; Sindbæk ed. 2022).

At Kaupang, the first site period may indeed have been brief and more seasonal, but settlement soon shifted into houses and repeatedly occupied plots. This sequence is valuable because it shows urbanization as a process rather than a binary label (Pilø 2007; Skre ed. 2007).

## 3.3 The city as “special economic zone”

Kalmring’s 2024 synthesis proposes a useful model: Viking towns were **spatially and juridically exceptional zones**, fostered because rulers wanted the advantages of trade without allowing unrestricted alien commerce to dissolve ordinary social controls. In this reading, towns concentrated strangers in places where a ruler’s representative could supervise disputes, defence, customs, and market activity. Birka’s separate assembly and local official, together with evidence from continental and Anglo-Saxon trading practice and later Scandinavian legal substrata, support parts of the model, although the exact content of town law remains incompletely known (Kalmring 2024, chs. 5, 7–9).

This is more convincing than either of two older extremes: that towns arose spontaneously wherever merchants happened to meet, or that kings designed every street from above. Urban growth could begin through network demand, but political power had strong incentives to **capture, protect, delimit, and tax** that growth.

# 4. The historical sequence of Viking urbanization

## 4.1 Before the towns

Before recognizably urban settlements developed, Viking society already possessed central places, elite residences, seasonal assemblies, cult sites, productive sites, and landing places. These could host exchange and specialized activity without supporting a permanent urban population. Corsi’s long-term study of Denmark stresses that later towns absorbed functions once distributed among such places (Corsi 2020).

This background matters because the city was not invented from nothing. It bundled existing practices into a concentrated and increasingly permanent settlement. Assembly, cult, elite patronage, maritime landing, craft production, redistribution, and regional exchange all had pre-urban histories.

## 4.2 The eighth-century maritime emporia

Ribe emerged around 700 CE and is now securely the earliest Scandinavian settlement commonly described as a town. By the later eighth and early ninth centuries an interconnected family of maritime emporia included Ribe, Birka, Hedeby/Groß Strömkendorf in the southern Baltic zone, and Kaupang. These were not replicas of one another but they shared a striking concentration of crafts and long-distance traffic (Croix 2015; Sindbæk 2007b; Richards 2026).

Network theory has transformed their interpretation. Sindbæk argues that early towns were **nodal points** in relatively sparse communication networks. Their importance lay not in dominating a huge local population but in linking otherwise distant clusters of exchange. Once merchants knew that particular nodes reliably offered partners, information, repair, provisions, and onward connections, repeated routes became routinized (Sindbæk 2007a, 2007b).

## 4.3 Ninth-century consolidation

During the ninth century, permanent occupation, specialized craft, silver exchange, fortifications, and elite interest became more visible at several sites. Hedeby grew into one of the greatest Baltic-North Sea transfer points. Kaupang connected Norwegian coastal resources with routes toward Denmark and the continent. Birka tied the Mälaren region to the Baltic and eastern routes. Ribe linked the North Sea world to Scandinavia (Skre 2007; Kalmring 2024; Sindbæk eds. 2022–2023).

The political significance of controlling such nodes is dramatically illustrated by the annalistic tradition that King Godfred forcibly transferred merchants from Reric to Hedeby in 808. The episode cannot tell us the everyday terms of settlement, but it shows that rulers understood merchant populations as **movable strategic assets** whose location could be manipulated for political and fiscal advantage (Kalmring 2024).

## 4.4 Viking conquest and the urban West

York and Dublin demonstrate a different route to Viking urbanism. They were embedded in conquered or contested landscapes with pre-existing local populations and institutions. York reused the Roman and Anglian urban fabric while developing an extraordinary Anglo-Scandinavian craft economy at sites such as Coppergate. Dublin began as a defended ship base in the ninth century and developed, especially after the return of Viking power in 917, into a dense, plotted, manufacturing and trading city (Hadley and ten Harkel eds. 2013; Wallace 2016; Reilly 2024).

These cities make nonsense of an ethnic model in which “Viking town” means a homogeneous Scandinavian enclave. Urban archaeology instead shows **hybrid communities**, technological exchange, local continuity, immigrant innovation, and household practices that could combine traditions from multiple regions.

## 4.5 The tenth- and eleventh-century transformation

From roughly 950 onward, the urban landscape changed again. Stronger kingships, domestic coinages, fortifications, ecclesiastical institutions, and administrative functions became more prominent. Corsi interprets the new Danish towns of the tenth and eleventh centuries as combining commerce with political administration and, increasingly, Christianity. Earlier emporia did not simply “evolve” into every later town: some declined or were replaced, while new foundations such as Sigtuna, Roskilde, and Trondheim embodied a changing political order (Corsi 2020; Richards 2026).

The urban history of the Viking Age is thus **not a straight line from market to medieval city**. It is a sequence of experiments in concentrating people, power, exchange, and institutions.

# 5. The physical city: plots, houses, streets, harbours, and boundaries

## 5.1 The plot was the basic urban cell

The most important spatial unit of Viking city life was often not the individual house but the **plot or tenement**. A plot might contain a dwelling, yard, workshop activity, storage, fence lines, a path, smaller structures, waste, and access to the street or waterfront. It therefore organized household identity and economic practice simultaneously (Boyd 2013; Pilø 2007; Reilly 2024).

At Kaupang, the central settlement has been reconstructed as roughly **90–100 small plots**, commonly around **40–80 m²**, normally with one principal building and limited exterior working space. The settlement’s central plot-divided zone was continuously used, while peripheral areas appear less permanent. This compactness helps explain why craft and domestic deposits overlap so strongly: there was simply little room to separate life into modern functional zones (Pilø 2007; McGraw and Mjærum 2024).

Dublin offers even more spectacular continuity. Fishamble Street and adjacent excavations revealed long, narrow properties marked by post-and-wattle fences. In one excavation, three larger properties were reorganized in the early tenth century into six plots, each containing a house, pathway, and ancillary structures; over successive phases, more than one hundred buildings were recorded. Elsewhere, plot alignments persisted from the tenth through eleventh centuries and in some cases influenced much later topography (Simpson 1998; Wallace 2016; Reilly 2024).

## 5.2 Houses were multifunctional

A Viking urban house was not merely a place to sleep after “going to work.” At Ribe, microstratigraphic analysis of a house shows one phase dominated by domestic activities such as food preparation, storage, sitting/sleeping, and weaving, followed by a phase in which metalworking was added. The sequence demonstrates how the same household space could shift economic function through time (Trant et al. 2024; Sindbæk ed. 2022).

Dublin’s common Hiberno-Norse house type was low and rectangular, usually with doors at opposite ends, a central passage or floor strip, raised side areas that served as benches or beds, and a central hearth. Its narrow form fitted the elongated urban plot exceptionally well. The form was therefore both architecture and urban adaptation (Wallace 2016; Reilly 2024).

## 5.3 Streets, paths, and collective order

Streets were expensive social facts. A route that persists while neighbouring houses are repeatedly rebuilt suggests collective agreement or an authority capable of preventing households from simply building wherever they pleased. Hedeby is a strong example: geophysics and excavation reveal planned paths and plots close to the shore. A major north-south path parallel to the waterfront has a regularity that researchers have interpreted as evidence for maintenance by some higher authority rather than by adjoining households independently (Kalmring 2024; Wichmann et al. 2025).

Road surfacing varied. Timber, brushwood, gravel, packed organic material, and later stone could be used. In wet settlements, maintaining passable movement surfaces was a constant struggle because organic refuse, trampling, rain, animal waste, and rebuilding raised ground levels. York’s and Dublin’s deep stratigraphy is in part the archaeological fossil of that continual urban accumulation (Addyman 1989; Reilly 2024).

## 5.4 Harbours were not empty edges

The harbour was the city’s front door, warehouse threshold, customs opportunity, workplace, and information exchange. Hedeby preserves jetties and ship remains; its shoreline plots appear to have extended toward wooden landing stages, implying that at least some waterfront facilities were associated with particular plots rather than being completely communal. The inference is important: ship unloading could be spatially allocated, linking specific households or commercial groups directly to the water (UNESCO Hedeby-Danevirke; Wichmann et al. 2025).

Kaupang, Birka, Dublin, Ribe, Truso, Wolin, and Ladoga likewise depended on direct navigable access. Their location is not incidental. Urbanization followed **maritime and riverine movement**, because bulk transport over water was cheaper and more reliable than dragging heavy cargo overland (Sindbæk 2007b; Kalmring 2024).

## 5.5 Boundaries mattered socially

Ditches, ramparts, fences, shorelines, and other limits were more than military architecture. They could mark where different rules, obligations, protection, or market practices applied. Some urban enclosures were too slight to function primarily as fortifications; their value may have been to define the settlement as a jurisdictional and fiscal space (Kalmring 2024; Corsi 2020).

That insight helps answer a basic question: **what did it mean to live “in town”?** It meant more than physical proximity. It placed a household inside a spatial system of neighbours, paths, market access, political authority, and repeated transactions.

# 6. Who ruled Viking cities?

## 6.1 There was no single Viking municipal constitution

Viking cities existed under different political conditions. Birka was associated with kings in the Mälaren world; Kaupang lay beside the power centre of Skiringssal; Hedeby occupied a contested Danish-border zone; Ribe developed within changing Danish rulership; York could be a royal capital; Dublin became the centre of a Scandinavian and Hiberno-Scandinavian kingdom. It is therefore misleading to imagine one uniform office of “mayor” or one town charter (Skre 2007; Corsi 2020; Kalmring 2024).

Nevertheless, recurring patterns appear: **royal or elite patronage, a local representative, collective assembly, protection, justice, customs, minting, defence, and control of market space**.

## 6.2 Kings and elite patrons

Rulers benefited from towns in at least four ways. First, customs and dues converted trade into revenue. Second, access to imported prestige goods and silver strengthened political gift economies. Third, control of harbours gave strategic leverage over mobility. Fourth, rulers could use towns to project authority into zones populated by foreigners and professional traders who were less embedded in local kinship structures (Kalmring 2024; Skre 2008).

The story of Godfred’s relocation of merchants from Reric to Hedeby in 808, whatever its narrative limitations, shows the political logic starkly: a merchant community could be treated as a resource whose relocation redirected commerce toward a ruler’s preferred node.

## 6.3 Officials and reeves

Rimbert’s *Vita Anskarii* names **Herigar** as a local prefect at Birka. In a crisis, Herigar remained with merchants and townspeople while the king was away. Elsewhere in the narrative, royal permission and local collective decision-making shape missionary activity. The text does not hand us a constitutional manual, but it does demonstrate a city in which royal power, a local official, merchants, and an assembly were distinct actors (Rimbert, *Vita Anskarii*).

Kalmring interprets such evidence comparatively, arguing that a king’s reeve or analogous officer could combine judicial, defensive, and customs functions. This is plausible and well grounded as a model, but because contemporary Scandinavian documentation is thin, it must not be turned into a claim that every town possessed an identical standardized office (Kalmring 2024, ch. 8).

## 6.4 The thing and collective urban decision-making

Birka appears to have had its own assembly context, and assemblies were fundamental to Viking political culture. In an urban setting a thing could provide a venue for disputes among merchants, enforcement of agreements, public decisions, and negotiation between inhabitants and political authority. Kalmring treats separate urban jurisdiction as a core feature distinguishing early towns from their surrounding countryside (Kalmring 2024, chs. 4–9).

Later town laws are tempting because they describe much more elaborate legal machinery, but they are chronologically dangerous. They can demonstrate the kinds of problems urban law had to solve, such as foreign merchants, market location, debt, property, weights, fines, and public maintenance, without proving that an eighth-century emporium used identical rules (Iversen 2017; Kalmring 2024).

## 6.5 Rule was strongest where rulers could capture flows

A ruler did not need to micromanage every house. The most profitable points of intervention were **boundaries, harbours, markets, mints, courts, and protected roads**. Control the place where ships unload, silver changes hands, disputes are judged, and strangers congregate, and much of the economic flow becomes legible and taxable.

This is why Viking cities should not be understood as islands of modern “free trade.” They were more plausibly **protected trading spaces inside narrow political boundaries**, where economic freedom was valuable precisely because an authority could delimit and profit from it (Kalmring 2024).

# 7. Who owned the ground, and how did someone obtain a house or plot?

This is one of the most important questions in Viking urban history and one of the easiest to answer badly. Archaeology gives us abundant evidence for **bounded plots, durable property lines, repeated rebuilding, regulated access, and organized waterfronts**. It gives us very little direct evidence for the exact legal form by which a particular family acquired the right to occupy one of those plots. Any confident statement that “the king owned all urban land,” that plots were freely bought and sold like modern real estate, or that urban householders held hereditary freehold is stronger than the evidence allows.

## 7.1 What we can demonstrate

Several sites show that land was **parcelled before or during early occupation**, rather than divided haphazardly after generations of settlement. Ribe’s early settlement north of the river included a main street with plots arranged on either side soon after activity began. Kaupang’s central area was divided into numerous small tenements. Hedeby’s shore zone shows repeated plot boundaries and organized paths. Dublin’s tenth-century city was divided into narrow fenced properties, sometimes reorganized from larger earlier holdings (Sindbæk ed. 2022; Pilø 2007; Simpson 1998; Reilly 2024).

This means somebody had the authority to say, in effect: **this space belongs to this household’s use, that space to another, this strip remains a route, and that edge remains waterfront access**. Whether that “somebody” was a ruler, royal official, landholding elite, corporate group of inhabitants, or some combination could vary by site and phase.

Durability is equally important. At Hedeby some property boundaries were renewed on essentially the same alignment for approximately a century. Dublin’s boundaries could survive through many rebuilding phases. A line that remains socially recognized after the fence itself rots is not merely a construction feature. It represents a remembered right or obligation (Wichmann et al. 2025; Wallace 2016; Reilly 2024).

## 7.2 Royal or elite ownership: probable in some cases, not universally proven

The regularity of Kaupang’s plots has been interpreted by some scholars as evidence for **royal or other high-status ownership of the underlying ground**. This is a reasonable inference because Kaupang was closely linked to the elite centre at Skiringssal and because laying out numerous uniform plots requires authority. Yet even a contemporary reviewer of the Kaupang volumes framed this as a likelihood rather than a demonstrated deed of ownership (Molaug 2009; Skre ed. 2007).

The distinction between **ownership of ground** and **rights of occupation** is crucial. A ruler or magnate could conceivably control the site while individual households held durable, perhaps inheritable or transferable, rights to specific plots. That structure is common in many premodern towns, but for Viking emporia it remains a model rather than a universally documented legal fact.

Kalmring’s broader interpretation makes royal or princely control plausible because the town itself was politically useful as a delimited, taxable trading zone. If rulers established or protected special market jurisdictions, they had a strong interest in controlling who occupied the most valuable commercial space (Kalmring 2024).

## 7.3 Did householders own their houses?

A house and the ground beneath it need not have had the same legal status. Timber, wattle, turf, and other building materials were labour investments made by the household occupying a plot, and structures were repeatedly rebuilt. It is therefore entirely possible that a household “owned” or controlled its building while occupying land subject to a superior lord, town authority, or inherited plot right. The surviving evidence rarely lets us separate these categories.

What archaeology does show is **household continuity and investment**. People repaired fences, resurfaced paths, rebuilt structures, reorganized workshops, and accumulated possessions. This was not a transient campground. A permanent resident needed enough security of tenure to justify repeated labour in the same place (Boyd 2013; Skre 2007; Reilly 2024).

## 7.4 How could a newcomer move into town?

No universal admission procedure survives. The best reconstruction is that entry occurred through several overlapping routes:

- **Household and kin connection.** A person might join an established urban household through marriage, kinship, service, apprenticeship, partnership, or dependency.
- **Commercial connection.** A travelling merchant or craftsperson could develop a recurring seasonal presence and later establish permanent residence if space and political permission allowed.
- **Patronage.** A ruler or elite patron could attract or relocate skilled producers and merchants. The Reric-Hedeby tradition is the extreme example.
- **Vacancy or transfer.** Plots and houses necessarily changed occupants as households died out, relocated, divided, or merged. Durable plot lines do not require durable families.
- **Expansion or replanning.** Dublin’s subdivision of larger properties into smaller plots shows that authorities or communities could create new occupancies by reorganizing space (Simpson 1998).
- **Institutional relocation.** Clerics, royal retainers, soldiers, mint workers, and other people connected to expanding tenth- and eleventh-century state structures could be placed in towns as part of political development (Corsi 2020).

What we should **not** assume is an unrestricted right of any outsider to arrive, stake out a rectangle, build a house, and thereby acquire ownership. Planned plots and controlled harbours argue against that.

## 7.5 Foreigners: welcome, useful, and potentially controlled

Viking towns required outsiders. Imported craft techniques, commodities, silver, and maritime expertise could not circulate without them. Birka, Ribe, Kaupang, Hedeby, York, Dublin, Sigtuna, Truso, Wolin, and Ladoga all show populations or material connections extending far beyond their immediate hinterlands (Croix et al. 2020; Krzewińska et al. 2018; Skre ed. 2011; Sindbæk ed. 2023).

Yet commercial usefulness did not eliminate political suspicion. Kalmring argues that early towns solved this tension by concentrating alien merchants inside bounded jurisdictions where they could trade under protection while remaining visible to the ruler’s officers. Even where that model cannot be demonstrated in every detail, it fits the combination of cosmopolitan population and strong spatial regulation (Kalmring 2024).

## 7.6 Could plots be inherited?

Long-lived boundaries make inheritance **possible but not demonstrable in most individual cases**. A plot could remain fixed while occupants changed. Conversely, an occupational family might persist for generations without leaving biological evidence. Archaeology can therefore identify continuity of *tenure units* more readily than continuity of *families*.

The safest formulation is: Viking urban plots could possess **durable social identities**, sometimes surviving repeated rebuilding for decades or generations. The mechanism by which those rights passed between people probably included inheritance, household succession, patronal allocation, and negotiated transfer, but we lack enough contemporary documentation to quantify each mechanism.

## 7.7 Rent, dues, and taxes

If superior ownership existed, rent is plausible; if a market jurisdiction existed, tolls and customs are highly plausible. These are not the same thing. A merchant might owe a customs duty for goods entering the market without renting a plot. A resident householder might owe service or payment connected to land occupancy. A ship might pay or negotiate harbour dues. Unfortunately, surviving evidence rarely lets us reconstruct a complete fiscal schedule.

Kalmring argues that customs collection was likely a primary function of royal officials in merchant towns. The wider European context strongly supports rulers monetizing protected trade. Coinage and minting later made such revenues easier to standardize, but early payments could also be rendered in weighed silver or goods (Kalmring 2024; Graham-Campbell ed. 2018).

### Bottom line on property

**High confidence:** plots were real, bounded, durable, organized, and socially regulated.  
**Moderate confidence:** rulers or elites often possessed superior authority over urban ground and could influence allocation.  
**Moderate confidence:** households held recognized occupation rights sufficiently secure for rebuilding and investment.  
**Low-to-moderate confidence:** specific mechanisms such as rent, inheritance, purchase, and subletting existed in forms comparable to later practice.  
**Low confidence:** any claim of uniform Viking-Age freehold, leasehold, citizenship, or royal ownership across all towns.

# 8. The urban household as home, workshop, shop, and firm

## 8.1 The household was the fundamental economic institution

A modern person tends to imagine urban work through separate businesses: a smithy, a shop, a warehouse, a home. Viking archaeology repeatedly dissolves those boundaries. The **household plot** was usually the basic unit in which labour, reproduction, storage, consumption, learning, exchange, and social life were combined (Boyd 2013; Sindbæk ed. 2022; Reilly 2024).

This is one of the most consequential conclusions of recent scholarship. It means occupational labels such as “merchant,” “weaver,” or “metalworker” describe **activities and skills** more securely than exclusive identities. A household might weave cloth for itself, produce a surplus, repair equipment, host a visiting trader, keep weighing equipment, store imported goods, and have one member who performed highly specialized metalwork. The economic unit was flexible.

## 8.2 Household composition

The urban household could include a married couple, children, older relatives, servants, dependants, apprentices, hired labour, guests, partners, and enslaved people. Archaeology rarely permits us to count these categories house by house. Nor should every building be assumed to contain a nuclear family. However, architecture and artefacts make clear that women, men, and children inhabited the same production spaces and that households were central to social reproduction (Hadley and ten Harkel eds. 2013).

Household size probably varied with wealth, occupation, and building type. A merchant household could accommodate assistants and visitors. A craft household could include learners. An elite compound might contain retainers. A tiny Kaupang plot constrained the number of permanent occupants differently from a larger Dublin property.

## 8.3 Work did not begin at a workplace door

Morning activity began around the hearth: rekindling fire, preparing food, fetching water, tending animals if present, opening storage, and setting out tools. Textile production could continue while other household tasks were performed. A craftsperson might work inside during poor weather and outside when heat, light, smoke, or hazardous debris made that preferable. Merchants weighed metal or sorted goods within domestic space. Children moved through the same environment and learned skills by proximity long before any formal apprenticeship can be documented.

Ribe’s microscopic house-floor research is especially revealing because chemical and sediment signatures separate zones of sitting/sleeping, food preparation, storage, weaving, and later metalworking. A house could literally change its economic personality while remaining a home (Trant et al. 2024).

## 8.4 Storage was capital

A trader’s wealth was not useful if it rotted, was stolen, or could not be sorted. Urban plots therefore required storage for food, raw materials, finished products, tools, fuel, textiles, imported pottery, silver, and personal possessions. Chests, boxes, suspended storage, bags, barrels, and small outbuildings are archaeologically uneven because organic containers decay, but preserved wood from Dublin, York, and Ribe makes the material world of storage unusually visible.

Barrels were economically important beyond holding liquids. Ribe’s network evidence includes imported wine-barrel wood and reused timber. Cooperage linked shipping, storage, and craft. Containers themselves circulated as valuable objects (Sindbæk ed. 2023).

## 8.5 The yard as production space

A small open area could host processes too dirty, smoky, bulky, or dangerous for the main living space: smithing, metal casting, antler sawing, hide scraping, waste sorting, animal slaughter, woodworking, or temporary storage. Production debris is therefore often distributed across plot interiors rather than confined to a single purpose-built workshop (Ashby and Sindbæk eds. 2020).

The yard also mediated between private household and public street. Customers, suppliers, neighbours, and passing traders could interact without entering the intimate core of the dwelling.

## 8.6 Household reputation and creditworthiness

Direct documents for Viking commercial credit are scarce, but repeated exchange inevitably required **trust**. A person dealing in weighed silver needed confidence that weights were acceptable. A craftsperson needed buyers to believe in quality. A merchant leaving goods with a partner needed confidence in future performance. In a town, reputation could travel faster because the same people met repeatedly in dense networks (Gustin 2004; Skre ed. 2008).

This does not mean a modern credit system existed. It means that **social capital reduced transaction costs**. Kinship, oath, patronage, shared origin, neighbourhood, religion, and repeated dealings could all serve as trust mechanisms.

## 8.7 Household resilience

Urban households were exposed to fire, disease, market failure, political attack, shipwreck, raw-material shortages, and fluctuating silver supply. Their multifunctionality was therefore an advantage. A household able to combine craft, petty trade, textile production, storage, and seasonal mobility had more ways to survive than one dependent on a single narrow income stream.

This is another reason not to project modern job specialization backward. Urban specialization certainly existed, sometimes at very high technical levels, but it operated inside households whose economic portfolios could remain broad.

# 9. The people of the city: residents, migrants, visitors, and foreigners

## 9.1 Urban population was layered

At any moment a Viking city could contain several overlapping populations:

- long-term resident households;
- seasonal merchants;
- ship crews waiting on cargo or weather;
- visiting farmers and suppliers;
- travelling craftspeople;
- itinerant specialists;
- political retainers and warriors;
- royal or local officials;
- clergy and missionaries;
- envoys and messengers;
- enslaved captives in transit or residence;
- migrants seeking permanent settlement;
- children, servants, and dependants whose mobility followed a household rather than individual choice.

This population could expand during market periods and contract during winter or difficult sailing seasons. Early emporia may therefore have had a **permanent core plus a pulsating transient population** (Croix 2015; Kalmring 2024).

## 9.2 Ribe: local roots and wider affiliations

Croix, Frei, Sindbæk, and Søvsø’s 2020 strontium study of 21 early Ribe burials found that most sampled individuals had isotope values compatible with the broader local or regional environment, while a minority were clearly different. Burial practice nevertheless displayed cultural connections extending beyond present-day Denmark. The result is important because it resists an easy equation between “foreign-looking burial” and “foreign-born person” (Croix et al. 2020).

Ribe’s cosmopolitanism was therefore not simply a colony of outsiders. It was a locally rooted town intensely connected to distant people and objects.

## 9.3 Sigtuna: migration as an engine of later Viking urbanization

The Sigtuna genomic study is even more striking. Twenty-three ancient genomes and associated isotope evidence show an urban population with unusually high diversity and origins ranging from local to long-distance. Mobility was not confined to men: women were also mobile. The authors conclude that migration was integral to early northern urbanization (Krzewińska et al. 2018).

This has broader implications. If towns offered work, market opportunity, patronage, religious institutions, security, and social connection, they could attract migrants for reasons other than raiding or elite politics. Urbanization itself created a destination.

## 9.4 Birka: a meeting place in the Mälaren world

Birka’s estimated population of roughly 700–1,000 was surrounded by thousands of graves, a fortification system, harbour access, and a rich import record. Contemporary narrative explicitly refers to merchants and royal interaction. Its material culture connects the Baltic, continental Europe, and eastern routes (Price et al. 2019; Ambrosiani 2013, 2021; Rimbert).

Birka’s diversity should not be reduced to ethnicity. People could distinguish themselves by occupation, household, wealth, language, religion, legal status, patron, region, gender, and age. “Scandinavian” and “foreign” are only two axes in a much denser social field.

## 9.5 Kaupang: origins visible in things

*Things from the Town* uses ceramics, glass, stone, ornaments, textile equipment, and other artefacts to reconstruct the inhabitants’ connections. One household has been interpreted as associated with a Frisian merchant; Slavic-linked material also appears, though the residence status of every foreign-connected individual cannot be known. The key fact is that an exceptionally diverse artefact assemblage distinguishes Kaupang from ordinary settlements (Skre ed. 2011).

## 9.6 Dublin and York: urban hybridity

Dublin and York complicate any simple opposition between Viking settler and local population. At Coppergate, textile evidence has been interpreted as suggesting continuity of Anglo-Saxon female textile practice within an increasingly Scandinavian urban culture. In Dublin, Norse and Irish technologies coexist; the city developed a distinct Hiberno-Scandinavian material culture (Walton Rogers 1997; Hadley and ten Harkel eds. 2013; Wallace 2016).

The city was therefore an engine of **cultural recombination**. A technology did not have to remain ethnically “owned” once neighbours, spouses, apprentices, customers, and co-workers encountered it daily.

## 9.7 Language and practical multilingualism

Direct evidence for everyday multilingual conversation is limited, but the communication problem is unavoidable. Merchants dealing across Old Norse, Old English, Old Irish, Frisian, Slavic, Baltic, Finnic, Arabic-using, and other linguistic zones needed practical solutions. These could include bilingual individuals, interpreters, formulaic vocabulary, gestures, standardized weights, recognizable objects, trusted brokers, and repeated partnerships.

Standardized material practices can be understood partly as **technologies of communication**. A balance scale and accepted weight can communicate value where speech is imperfect. A familiar bead type, coin, or metal standard can reduce ambiguity between strangers (Skre ed. 2008; Ashby and Sindbæk eds. 2020).

# 10. Work in a Viking city

The word “work” is useful but potentially misleading. Viking people did not organize labour through modern job descriptions, fixed forty-hour weeks, or sharply separated sectors. Some people were highly specialized. Others combined many tasks. Seasonal rhythms mattered. Household members contributed according to skill, age, gender expectations, status, opportunity, and necessity. The following categories describe **documented urban activities**, not a modern occupational census.

## 10.1 Merchants and commercial brokers

Merchants ranged from people conducting long-distance voyages with high-value cargo to householders selling surplus or finished craft goods. A “merchant” might also own or share a ship, participate in warfare, maintain a farm elsewhere, or perform craft. Contemporary Birka narrative clearly recognizes merchants as a meaningful group, while scales, weights, imported goods, and silver show commercial practice archaeologically (Rimbert; Skre ed. 2008).

A long-distance merchant needed capital, information, partners, containers, transport, armed protection or political guarantees, and knowledge of local exchange customs. Much of the profession was therefore **relationship management** rather than simply buying low and selling high.

## 10.2 Shipmasters, sailors, pilots, and maritime labour

Every port depended on people who could navigate, sail, row, maintain hulls and rigging, handle cargo, assess weather, and find safe channels. Not all were permanent townspeople. Ship crews formed a transient labour force that consumed food and lodging while adding manpower, information, and security.

Harbour activity required loading and unloading heavy goods, carrying water, moving barrels, hauling animals, guarding cargo, repairing rope and sails, and arranging onward transport. These tasks leave less distinctive archaeological signatures than a metal workshop, so they are almost certainly **underrepresented** in occupational reconstructions.

## 10.3 Blacksmiths and ironworkers

Ironworking was indispensable. Tools, knives, nails, rivets, hinges, locks, ship fastenings, weapons, agricultural equipment, and repair all required smithing. Hammerscale, slag, forge debris, tuyères, and tools identify urban production at multiple sites. Blacksmithing varied from routine repair to highly skilled manufacture, and an individual smith’s repertoire could be broad (Ashby and Sindbæk eds. 2020).

Ironworkers also serviced other crafts. A woodworker needed edge tools; a leatherworker needed knives and awls; shipbuilders consumed enormous numbers of rivets. The smith therefore occupied a central position in the urban technological web.

## 10.4 Non-ferrous metalworkers, jewellers, and casters

Ribe preserves spectacular evidence for bronze and other non-ferrous metalworking, including moulds and crucibles. Metallurgical analysis indicates workshops stable enough for knowledge to persist across generations. Silver and gold working linked craft production directly to monetary and prestige economies because the same metals could be ornament, bullion, payment, and political gift (Croix, Neiß and Sindbæk 2019; Northern Emporium publications; Orfanou et al. 2021).

Highly skilled ornamental casting required alloy knowledge, mould preparation, controlled temperatures, design traditions, and access to imported or recycled metal. These crafts are among the clearest examples of genuinely specialized urban expertise.

## 10.5 Mint workers and moneyers

Where coinage was produced, minting created a politically controlled craft at the intersection of metallurgy, authority, and commerce. Ribe and Hedeby were early Scandinavian contexts for coin-based exchange; later royal mints became more important as kings standardized currency and taxation (Corsi 2020; Sindbæk ed. 2022).

Moneyers needed dies, metal preparation, striking skill, weight control, and official authorization. Their existence marks a substantial step from decentralized bullion exchange toward sovereign monetary power.

## 10.6 Glassworkers and beadmakers

Glass-bead production is one of Ribe’s signature crafts. Raw glass, cullet, failed beads, furnaces or hearth traces, and specialized tools document an industry connected to material moving across enormous distances. Northern Emporium research shows that glass types and bead styles changed through time as networks shifted (Sindbæk eds. 2022, 2023).

A bead was small, portable, visible, and socially meaningful. Beadmaking therefore converted imported raw material into high-value objects ideal for an urban network economy.

## 10.7 Combmakers, antler workers, and bone workers

Antler and bone were ubiquitous raw materials for combs, pins, gaming pieces, handles, fittings, and other items. Production waste is archaeologically conspicuous because sawn antler offcuts survive well. Combmaking could be highly standardized and mobile styles spread rapidly between towns (Ashby 2013; Ashby and Sindbæk eds. 2020).

Biomolecular identification of Ribe combs has shown that antler raw material could itself travel significant distances, revealing a supply chain behind an apparently ordinary personal object (Rey-Iglesia et al. 2023).

## 10.8 Woodworkers, carpenters, turners, and coopers

Wood was the skeleton of the city. Houses, fences, streets, jetties, carts, ships, chests, bowls, spoons, tool handles, barrels, furniture, and fuel all depended on it. Waterlogged York and Dublin preserve enough wood to expose a world normally invisible on dry sites (Wallace 2016; York Archaeological Trust publications).

Specialization probably varied. Building a house, turning a bowl, making a barrel watertight, and constructing a seagoing ship required overlapping but distinct skill sets. Cooperage was particularly significant for liquid cargo and bulk storage.

## 10.9 Shipwrights

Shipbuilding and major repair demanded advanced woodworking, iron fastenings, caulking materials, rope, sailcloth, and coordinated labour. Major shipyards are difficult to identify inside every town, but harbour cities necessarily supported at least repair capacity. Shipwrights connected urban craft to the maritime system on which the city depended.

## 10.10 Leatherworkers, shoemakers, and hide processors

Leather was used for footwear, belts, straps, bags, sheaths, harness, clothing components, and equipment. York and Dublin preserve large quantities of leather and offcuts. Hide preparation could be foul-smelling and water-intensive, so different stages may have been spatially separated, with finished leather brought into dense residential areas for cutting and stitching.

The abundance of footwear at waterlogged sites also reminds us that shoes were consumables. Muddy streets wore them out. Repair and replacement generated continual local demand.

## 10.11 Textile workers: spinning, weaving, dyeing, finishing, sewing

Textile production was one of the largest labour demands in the entire Viking economy. Clothing, blankets, bags, rope-like products, and above all sails consumed enormous quantities of fibre and time. Coppergate alone produced 1,147 textile-related artefacts, more than 1,000 dating to the ninth through thirteenth centuries, with the Anglo-Scandinavian period especially well represented. Evidence includes flax and wool, fibre-preparation tools, spindles and whorls, several loom types, dye plants, finishing tools, cutting equipment, and sewing implements (Walton Rogers 1997).

In early phases, textile work at York appears widely distributed across tenements, consistent with household production rather than centralized workshops. Increasing specialization becomes clearer later. This transition warns against assuming that every spindle whorl identifies a professional weaver (Walton Rogers 1997).

## 10.12 Tailors and clothing finishers

Cutting and fitted garment construction require skills distinct from spinning and weaving. Ribe’s craft assemblages include evidence associated with tailoring. A town concentrated consumers who could pay for specialized finishing, repair, and fashionable forms, making clothing a likely interface between household production and professional service.

## 10.13 Stone workers and whetstone supply

Stone entered towns as quern material, whetstones, building material, ballast, jewellery stone, vessels, and specialized raw material. Geological provenancing reveals long-distance supply. Norwegian whetstones in Slavic and Baltic zones show how a mundane sharpening tool could be embedded in far-reaching commerce.

## 10.14 Potters and pottery traders

Ceramic production differed regionally. Some Viking Scandinavian towns imported large proportions of their pottery rather than supporting a major local wheel-thrown industry, while York and continental-connected regions had different traditions. Pottery is therefore especially useful for tracking **interaction spheres and household habits**, not just identifying potters.

## 10.15 Food processors, butchers, fishers, and brewers

Urban food preparation generated specialized skills even when not full-time occupations. Butchery marks can show systematic carcass processing. Fish could arrive fresh, dried, salted, or smoked. Grain had to be milled. Brewing transformed grain into a safer and socially important drink. Baking technology varied, and it is unsafe to imagine a later medieval professional baker on every Viking street.

The economic point is that urban residents paid, traded, or reciprocated for **processed calories**, not merely raw crops. A cattle trader, butcher, cook, and hide worker could all participate in the value chain of a single animal.

## 10.16 Builders and maintenance labour

Dense wooden towns required continual repair. Roofs leaked. Wattle decayed. hearths needed rebuilding. Streets sank. Waterfronts eroded. Jetties broke. Defensive works required labour. Building maintenance was therefore not episodic; it was a continuous urban industry, though much of it may have been performed communally or by households rather than by full-time builders.

## 10.17 Porters, carriers, and animal handlers

Cargo does not walk from ship to house by itself. Urban economies required people to move barrels, sacks, timber, stone, animals, and packages through narrow lanes. Pack animals, carts where feasible, sledges in winter, and human backs all contributed. These workers are archaeologically quiet but economically indispensable.

## 10.18 Officials, guards, retainers, and legal specialists

Rulers required agents. A reeve or prefect, customs collectors, mint supervisors, guards, messengers, and retainers all performed work even if their income came through patronage, land, food support, fines, or shares of revenue rather than wages. Assemblies also depended on people who knew customary law and could mediate disputes (Rimbert; Kalmring 2024).

## 10.19 Clergy and religious specialists

Missionaries and later resident clergy added another occupational sphere. Churches required construction, maintenance, books and liturgical objects, food support, burial management, and connections to wider ecclesiastical networks. Before and alongside Christianity, ritual specialists and cult practices likewise intersected with exchange and political authority, although the urban archaeological visibility of pagan religious personnel is poor (Kalmring 2024; Corsi 2020).

## 10.20 Entertainers, musicians, and storytellers

Musical instruments from Ribe and other towns prove performance, but not a professional entertainment class. Urban density nevertheless created audiences: households, feasts, market gatherings, ship crews, and elite retinues. Storytelling, poetry, music, gaming, and drinking were part of social life even when performers earned their living in other ways (Sindbæk ed. 2023).

## 10.21 Servants, dependants, and enslaved workers

Much necessary labour leaves no occupational signature because it was performed inside dependency relationships: carrying water, tending fires, childcare, food preparation, cleaning, animal care, spinning, hauling, and assisting crafts. Free servants and enslaved persons may therefore be structurally invisible if we look only for specialized toolkits.

### Work in one sentence

The Viking city did not contain a row of modern businesses. It contained **households with different skill concentrations**, surrounded by mobile specialists, traders, officials, crews, dependants, and service labour, all connected by constant exchange.

# 11. Women, gender, and the urban economy

## 11.1 Women were economic actors, not merely residents

Any reconstruction that puts men in workshops and women inside a purely domestic sphere is inconsistent with the evidence. Textile production alone placed women, in many regions, at the centre of one of the most labour-intensive sectors of the economy. Spinning, weaving, dyeing, sewing, repair, and cloth finishing generated household necessities, exchangeable surplus, and the sails without which Viking maritime expansion was impossible (Walton Rogers 1997; Ashby and Sindbæk eds. 2020).

Gendered associations in graves and tools suggest that textile labour was strongly associated with women, though not every textile task was exclusively female and not every woman performed identical work. At Coppergate, Walton Rogers argued that incoming Scandinavian men may have entered a town where much textile production continued in the hands of local Anglo-Saxon women, providing a striking example of urban cultural continuity amid political transformation (Walton Rogers 1997).

## 11.2 Household management was economic management

Because household and firm overlapped, running a household meant controlling economic resources: stored food, cloth, fuel, keys, containers, labour, guests, and perhaps silver or goods. Keys in female graves have often been interpreted symbolically in relation to household authority; the exact social meaning varies, but household management should not be trivialized as “domestic” and therefore non-economic.

A merchant or craft household could not function if provisioning, textile production, storage, childcare, hospitality, and labour coordination were treated as economically irrelevant. Women’s work made urban specialization possible.

## 11.3 Women moved to towns

Sigtuna’s combined genomic and isotope evidence demonstrates significant female as well as male mobility. This matters because older migration models often imagined mobile men marrying static local women. Urban evidence is more complex. Women could move through marriage, kin networks, slavery, trade, religious affiliation, craft transmission, or household relocation (Krzewińska et al. 2018).

Early Ribe likewise cautions against equating material style with birthplace. A woman could participate in a culturally far-reaching urban community while being locally born, or migrate without retaining a visibly “foreign” material signature (Croix et al. 2020).

## 11.4 Were women merchants?

The evidence permits women’s participation in exchange but does not support a numerical estimate of “female merchants.” Household production necessarily involved marketing or distribution when goods were sold beyond the home. Women could control portable wealth and participate in property transmission. Later Scandinavian sources provide women with varying economic rights, but those cannot simply be projected unchanged into every eighth- or ninth-century town.

The safest conclusion is that **commerce was not socially isolated from women**, even if long-distance seaborne trading expeditions and some public offices were probably male dominated.

## 11.5 Gender was urbanly negotiated

Urban life brought people from different customary traditions into close contact. Clothing styles, jewellery, craft techniques, marriage networks, and burial customs could therefore become ways of negotiating identity. York and Dublin show especially clear hybridization. Gender roles were not erased by the city, but the city created new contexts in which those roles were performed, combined, and transmitted (Hadley and ten Harkel eds. 2013).

# 12. Children and growing up urban

## 12.1 Children were everywhere even when archaeology overlooks them

Children constituted a substantial portion of any premodern population. They lived in houses where blades, hearths, animals, craft waste, smoke, standing water, traffic, and open waterfronts were ordinary. Childhood in a Viking city therefore differed materially from childhood on an isolated farm, even though many household expectations were shared.

The archaeology of Dublin includes toys and child-associated objects that allow children to be treated as people who created their own social worlds rather than simply incomplete adults. Deirdre McAlister’s study of childhood in Viking and Hiberno-Scandinavian Dublin emphasizes environment, socialization, and play (McAlister 2013).

## 12.2 Learning happened in the household economy

Children living beside spinning, carving, metalwork, trade, food preparation, and ship repair absorbed techniques through observation and increasingly capable participation. We cannot identify a formal apprenticeship system for every Viking craft, but intergenerational transfer is the most plausible mechanism for many skills. Stable craft traditions at Ribe and other towns imply repeated teaching (Ashby and Sindbæk eds. 2020; Sindbæk ed. 2022).

A child’s contribution could begin with low-risk repetitive tasks: sorting material, tending animals, carrying small loads, twisting fibre, gathering fuel, fetching water, or helping with food. Skill acquisition could gradually become economically significant.

## 12.3 Play was also social training

Miniature weapons, boats, gaming objects, and other toys can blur the distinction between play and learning. Children rehearsed adult identities, household roles, competition, navigation, craft, or combat through play. Urban streets and yards also created peer groups larger and more diverse than those available on scattered farms (McAlister 2013).

## 12.4 Mortality and emotional value

Childhood mortality was high, but high mortality did not mean children were emotionally or socially unimportant. Carefully furnished children’s graves at Birka demonstrate family investment and the attribution of status and gendered identity to young individuals. One well-known child burial included jewellery and personal equipment indicative of a prosperous household (Swedish History Museum, “The Birka Girl”).

The broader methodological lesson is important: demographic fragility and emotional value can coexist.

# 13. Enslaved people and coerced labour

## 13.1 Slavery belonged to the Viking urban economy

Raiding, captive-taking, and slave trading are securely documented features of the Viking world. Towns were logical nodes for moving captives because they concentrated ships, merchants, silver, political protection, and long-distance routes. Ben Raffield’s comparative study argues that slave markets are archaeologically “invisible” not because the trade was unimportant but because human sale often requires little durable infrastructure (Raffield 2019).

Iron shackles and restraints from places including Dublin, Birka, and Hedeby demonstrate coercion, although an isolated restraint cannot by itself reconstruct a market. Written sources provide the complementary evidence for captives being taken and traded (Raffield 2019; *Annals of Ulster* and other annalistic sources).

## 13.2 Where were people sold?

We do **not** possess a securely excavated Viking equivalent of a later purpose-built slave market with stalls labelled by function. Sale could occur at the harbour, within a general market, through private negotiation, at an elite residence, or aboard or beside ships. Raffield emphasizes comparative possibilities rather than claiming a universally identifiable architectural type (Raffield 2019).

This is precisely the kind of topic where absence of a specialized building must not be mistaken for absence of an institution.

## 13.3 Enslaved people as urban labour

Once in a town, enslaved persons could perform domestic service, food preparation, childcare, carrying, textile work, craft assistance, animal care, loading, agricultural work in nearby estates, and sexual or personal service. The exact urban division of enslaved labour is difficult to detect because a slave using an ordinary spindle, knife, or bucket leaves the same object as a free worker.

No responsible reconstruction should invent “slave quarters” unless a particular site provides independent evidence. Legal status is often **materially silent**.

## 13.4 Slavery and mobility

Some of the enormous human mobility detected biologically in Viking communities was voluntary; some was not. Isotope or DNA analysis can show that a person grew up elsewhere but cannot by itself distinguish merchant, spouse, captive, migrant craftsperson, or traveller. Social interpretation therefore requires burial context and historical evidence, and even then uncertainty may remain (Croix et al. 2020; Krzewińska et al. 2018).

## 13.5 Manumission and status change

Later Scandinavian law shows pathways between slavery and freedom, but the details vary regionally and chronologically. It is plausible that freed people lived in towns and exploited urban opportunities, but direct eighth- or ninth-century evidence is limited. Urban anonymity and economic diversity may have created opportunities for status renegotiation, yet this remains an inference rather than a measurable demographic fact.

# 14. How the urban economy actually worked

## 14.1 The city did not produce everything it consumed

A defining characteristic of an urban economy is that many residents spend much of their labour on things other than growing their own food. That creates a daily problem: **how does the city eat tomorrow?** Skre has stressed that a non-food-producing urban population becomes dependent on frequent transactions. This transactional density itself encourages new economic behaviour (Skre 2008).

The urban economy can therefore be modelled as overlapping circulations rather than a single “trade” sector.

### Flow 1: Hinterland → city

Food, livestock, fish, grain, dairy products, fuel, timber, wool, hides, antler, iron, stone, and labour moved inward. Some came from nearby farms; other necessities travelled far. Birka’s imported cattle show that even subsistence could participate in regional networks (Schjerven et al. 2024).

### Flow 2: City → hinterland

Finished metalwork, jewellery, beads, combs, imported pottery, prestige objects, coins, repaired tools, information, and market services moved outward. Rural producers gained access to goods and currencies otherwise difficult to obtain.

### Flow 3: Long-distance network → city

Silver dirhams, glass, wine, ceramics, gemstones, metals, silk, spices or other rare materials, decorative objects, and people arrived from continental Europe, the British Isles, the Baltic, Scandinavia, and eastern river routes. Ribe’s Northern Emporium volumes trace raw materials and artefacts from Western Europe, Scandinavia, the Middle East, and even wider Indian Ocean-linked systems (Sindbæk ed. 2023).

### Flow 4: City → long-distance network

Furs, hides, antler products, amber, iron, whetstones, slaves, textiles, craft goods, and regional commodities left northern ports. The exact export basket varied by town and period.

### Flow 5: City → city

The network was not simply “town versus countryside.” Towns exchanged with other towns. Standardized objects, craft styles, metal, coins, weights, and technologies moved between urban nodes, making them a communication system (Sindbæk 2007a, 2007b; Ashby and Sindbæk eds. 2020).

## 14.2 Value was added inside the city

Urban crafts often converted imported or rural raw material into products with much higher value. Antler became combs. Glass became beads. Silver became jewellery. Iron became tools and weapons. Wool became cloth. Hides became shoes. Timber became ships, barrels, houses, and containers. This **value-added production** explains why crafts concentrate where markets and materials meet.

## 14.3 Market exchange and social exchange coexisted

Viking economies cannot be reduced either to impersonal markets or to gift exchange. Silver could be weighed for a purchase, hoarded as wealth, worn as ornament, gifted to secure loyalty, or cut into pieces for payment. Goods could circulate through kinship and patronage as well as sale. Graham-Campbell’s *Silver, Butter, Cloth* explicitly emphasizes the coexistence of monetary and social economies (Graham-Campbell ed. 2018).

The city was significant because it increased the frequency of dealings among people **outside close kin relations**, making standardized value and enforceable trust more important.

## 14.4 Prices and bargaining

Almost no complete Viking urban price list survives. Prices were probably negotiated according to quality, scarcity, relationship, season, and accepted units. Standard weights constrained bargaining by providing a shared measure of silver, but they did not create fixed prices.

A buyer and seller could therefore argue over two things: **how much the object was worth** and **whether the payment was honest**. Scales, weights, touch, sound, metal quality, coin recognition, and reputation all mattered.

## 14.5 Taxation and customs

Political authorities had strong incentives to take a share of concentrated commerce. Kalmring argues that selected towns were granted distinctive legal status precisely because rulers could protect and tax long-distance exchange there. Customs may have been assessed on goods, ships, market transactions, or access, though specific schedules are poorly preserved for the early period (Kalmring 2024).

Taxation need not suppress commerce. Protection, standardized dispute resolution, and safe harbour could make a taxed market more attractive than a theoretically untaxed but dangerous one.

## 14.6 Urban economic risk

The economy was vulnerable to:

- shipwreck and weather;
- piracy and warfare;
- political closure of routes;
- failure of silver supply;
- crop failure in the hinterland;
- fire;
- epidemic disease;
- loss of political protection;
- harbour silting or environmental change;
- competition from a new town;
- deliberate royal relocation of commerce.

The abandonment of major early emporia and rise of successor towns demonstrate that urban success was not permanent. A city existed because networks continued to choose it.

# 15. Money, silver, weighing, barter, and commodity payment

## 15.1 There was no single Viking monetary system

Viking exchange used a spectrum from direct commodity exchange to weighed bullion to coin. The balance among these changed across time and region. The old picture of “barter until coins arrived” is wrong. Weighed silver was money in a functional sense even when pieces were not minted as local currency (Skre ed. 2008; Graham-Campbell ed. 2018).

## 15.2 Kaupang: economic change under a microscope

The Kaupang silver assemblage is among the best studied. In the early ninth century, silver arrived mainly through Carolingian connections and was used on a relatively limited scale with locally produced lead weights. From the later ninth century, imported Islamic dirhams and weight systems of probable Islamic inspiration transformed payment practices. The settlement produced hundreds of weights as well as balances, hacksilver, ingots, and coins (Skre ed. 2008).

The significance is behavioural: **the city encouraged people to think transactionally**. A rural landowner might exchange within long-standing relationships; a craftsperson selling to strangers needed divisible, measurable value.

## 15.3 Hacksilver

Silver objects could be cut into fragments and weighed. A brooch, ingot, coin, arm-ring, or scrap could therefore move between categories of adornment, savings, payment, and raw material. “Hacksilver economy” should not be mistaken for primitive randomness. It required careful weighing and knowledge of metal quality (Hårdh in Skre ed. 2008).

## 15.4 Coins

Coins could function by count, by weight, as bullion, or as politically meaningful tokens. Early Scandinavian minting at Ribe and Hedeby represents a major institutional development because coinage links authority to value standardization. Yet foreign coins, especially Islamic dirhams, continued to circulate far beyond the political reach of the rulers who minted them (Graham-Campbell ed. 2018; Corsi 2020).

## 15.5 Commodity money

Silver was not the only transferable standard of value. Cloth, butter, and other commodities could serve monetary functions in parts of the Viking world. Commodity payment is especially rational where silver is scarce and where goods have standardized, widely recognized usefulness (Graham-Campbell ed. 2018).

## 15.6 Barter

Direct exchange of one good for another certainly occurred, but “barter economy” is too crude a label. A seller might price in silver while accepting equivalent goods. A debt might be denominated in one unit and settled in another. A relationship could combine gifts, obligations, and purchases.

## 15.7 Counterfeit, clipping, and trust

Whenever value becomes portable, fraud becomes possible. Standard weights, public markets, known moneyers, reputation, and legal jurisdiction all reduce the risk of being cheated. This is another way political authority and economic growth reinforce one another.

# 16. Food and provisioning: feeding a non-farming population

## 16.1 The metabolic problem of the town

Every day a Viking city converted rural calories into urban labour. Unlike a farmstead, a dense plot could not support enough arable land or pasture to feed its occupants. Food supply was therefore not background logistics. It was the **foundation of urban existence**.

Zooarchaeology at Ribe, Birka, York, Dublin, and Kaupang shows cattle, sheep/goats, pigs, fish, birds, and wild resources in varying proportions. Plant remains document cereals and gathered or cultivated foods. Preservation differences prevent simple cross-site ranking, but the overall picture is one of mixed diets sustained by exchange.

## 16.2 Livestock could arrive alive

Birka’s 2024 isotope study shows cattle potentially raised more than 180 km from the town while pigs and sheep/goats were more local. Live cattle are economically useful because they transport themselves, preserve meat until slaughter, and provide hide, horn, bone, fat, and other products after death (Schjerven et al. 2024).

Driving animals to town also transferred nutrients and waste into the urban environment. Slaughter therefore connected provisioning to sanitation, craft raw materials, and street ecology.

## 16.3 Some animals were kept in town

Evidence from Birka suggests pigs may have been raised within the settlement, while other livestock products entered as surplus. Small-scale urban animal keeping would have been practical for pigs, poultry, and perhaps short-term holding of larger stock. It also made towns noisier, dirtier, and more biologically complex.

## 16.4 Grain, bread, porridge, and ale

Cereals supplied durable calories and could be transported in bulk. Grain needed storage protected from moisture and pests, then grinding and cooking. Porridge and bread forms varied by region and technology. Ale production consumed grain but provided a socially important drink used in hospitality and gathering.

Urban brewing could occur at household scale. Evidence does not justify placing a commercial tavern on every street, even though drinking and hospitality were certainly central to social life.

## 16.5 Fish

Fish were ideal urban commodities because they could be brought fresh from nearby waters or preserved for longer transport. Marine, river, and lake fisheries connected cities to specialized producers. Fish bones in urban deposits also help reconstruct seasonality and trade.

## 16.6 Dairy

Milk itself travels poorly without containers and careful handling, but butter and cheese concentrate calories and preserve them. Their importance varied regionally. Commodity-money traditions involving butter illustrate how food and value could overlap (Graham-Campbell ed. 2018).

## 16.7 Famine and price stress

Urban dependence created vulnerability. A failed harvest or blocked route could produce rapid scarcity because many townspeople had little direct food production to fall back on. Political authorities therefore had incentives to protect approaches and maintain reliable markets even when no formal grain policy is documented.

# 17. Shipping, harbour work, transport, and information

## 17.1 The harbour was an economic machine

A Viking harbour required far more than ships. Cargo had to be scheduled, sorted, carried, guarded, stored, taxed, repaired, sold, and transferred. Crew needed food and water. Hulls needed patching. Rigging and sails wore out. Animals had to be led ashore. Waste had to go somewhere. Every arrival created a burst of labour.

Hedeby’s excavated and surveyed harbour, including jetties and ship remains, makes this infrastructure visible. At other towns, shoreline alteration and waterfront rebuilding reveal repeated investment in access (UNESCO Hedeby-Danevirke; Wallace 2016).

## 17.2 Water transport shaped urban geography

Ribe sits on a river connected to the North Sea; Birka on Lake Mälaren with access toward the Baltic; Hedeby on the Schlei near the narrowest transfer between Baltic and North Sea systems; Kaupang on the Norwegian coast; Dublin on the Liffey; York on the Ouse/Foss system; Ladoga on the Volkhov route. These locations are not simply scenic. They minimize the cost of moving heavy material.

## 17.3 Portage and transfer nodes

Hedeby’s importance was magnified by its position near an overland transfer between maritime systems. A cargo could be moved across a relatively narrow land corridor rather than sailing around Jutland. Such transshipment points create demand for carts, animals, porters, warehouses, and brokers.

## 17.4 Ships carried information

Every ship delivered news: prices, wars, deaths, political changes, safe routes, new fashions, religious ideas, and rumours. A town was therefore an **information market**. Merchants who knew which harbour had silver, which ruler had closed a route, or which craft style was fashionable possessed economic advantage.

This informational function helps explain why towns remained valuable even when a particular cargo could theoretically be exchanged elsewhere. Network centrality compounds itself: people go where they expect to meet people who know things (Sindbæk 2007a).

## 17.5 Seasonality

Northern sailing conditions created seasonal rhythms. Spring and summer brought intensified movement; winter could reduce long-distance traffic while increasing repair and local production. A resident craft household therefore experienced the year differently from an itinerant merchant. Urban population and cash/silver availability likely fluctuated accordingly.

## 17.6 The city as network switchboard

The best metaphor is not a warehouse but a **switchboard**. A good Viking town allowed cargo, money, people, and information arriving on one route to be redirected onto another. Ribe linked North Sea and Scandinavian networks; Hedeby linked Baltic and North Sea systems; Birka linked the Mälaren basin with Baltic and eastern routes; Ladoga connected Baltic traffic with river routes into the Rus world (Sindbæk 2023; Grigorjeva 2022).

# 18. Social life, identity, trust, and neighbourhood

## 18.1 Urban density changed social experience

Most Vikings lived in dispersed agrarian settings. A town compressed strangers, neighbours, clients, competitors, visitors, and dependants into a walkable space. People encountered others not only at seasonal assemblies or feasts but every day: at the street edge, harbour, well, workshop, market, hearth, and fence line. That frequency created both opportunity and friction (Hadley and ten Harkel eds. 2013; Kalmring 2024).

Urban social identity was therefore multidimensional. A person could be known as a member of a household, a Frisian or Dane, a smith, a wealthy merchant, a Christian, a retainer of a particular ruler, a recent migrant, a local-born inhabitant, a widow, a shipmaster, or someone from a particular plot or neighbourhood. These identities could overlap rather than compete.

## 18.2 Neighbours mattered

A fence did not isolate a household. Smoke, noise, sparks, smells, animals, waste water, traffic, and fire crossed plot boundaries. So did gossip, assistance, tools, customers, children, and danger. Repeated boundaries at Hedeby and Dublin indicate long-term spatial neighbourhoods in which adjoining households had to negotiate collective problems (Wallace 2016; Reilly 2024; Wichmann et al. 2025).

The maintenance of a lane is a social act. So is the decision not to extend a wall into it. Even without a written ordinance, urban life required shared expectations.

## 18.3 Hospitality and lodging

Travelling merchants and crews needed places to sleep and eat. Some probably stayed aboard ship; others were hosted by partners, relatives, patrons, or local households. Purpose-built inns are not securely documented for every Viking town, and the Latin/Germanic ancestry of words related to traders and innkeepers should not be converted automatically into a street of commercial taverns. What is certain is that **hospitality was economic infrastructure**.

Hosting a stranger created information flow and commercial trust. It could also impose obligations. In a society where personal relationships helped secure trade, a meal and sleeping place could be part of business.

## 18.4 Drinking, feasting, and conversation

Ale and feasting were central to Viking social life. In towns, such gatherings could bind craft households, crews, patrons, and neighbours. Feasting also transformed surplus food into reputation and alliance. Imported wine, attested materially in northern trade networks, carried prestige beyond its caloric value (Sindbæk ed. 2023).

The absence of a securely identified “pub district” should not erase drinking. It should simply keep us from imposing later commercial architecture on earlier practice.

## 18.5 Gaming and leisure

Gaming pieces and boards are widespread Viking finds. Games provided recreation, competition, social display, and perhaps gambling. Musical instruments from Ribe and elsewhere add performance to the picture. Urban leisure was not separate from work in the modern weekend sense; music, games, story, drink, bargaining, and craft could share the same household or market gathering (Sindbæk ed. 2023).

## 18.6 Conflict and dispute

Density produces quarrels: unpaid obligations, damaged property, dishonest weights, insults, theft, violence, sexual rivalry, inheritance conflict, and commercial disputes. Urban jurisdiction mattered because strangers could not always rely on shared kin groups to mediate. A local assembly or ruler’s representative provided a mechanism for making agreements enforceable beyond the household (Kalmring 2024).

## 18.7 Reputation as infrastructure

In the absence of credit bureaus, corporate registries, or standardized contracts, **reputation travelled through people**. A merchant known to cheat could lose partners. A craftsperson whose combs broke or metal was debased could lose customers. A household known for fair hospitality could attract recurring relationships.

Urban anonymity existed only in relative terms. A town of hundreds or a few thousand people, organized into stable plots, could be socially dense enough that behaviour became widely known.

# 19. Religion, ritual, and Christianization

## 19.1 Towns were religious contact zones

Early Viking cities gathered people who did not necessarily share identical ritual practices. Local Scandinavian cult, regional deities, amulets, burial traditions, Christian missionaries, foreign Christians, and later churches could coexist. The city was therefore a place where religious difference became a daily social fact rather than a distant abstraction (Rimbert; Kalmring 2024; Corsi 2020).

## 19.2 Birka and missionary Christianity

Rimbert’s account of Ansgar’s missions to Birka is invaluable precisely because it situates religious change within urban politics. Missionaries require royal permission, interact with a local official, encounter merchants and townspeople, and depend on collective decisions. Christianity entered not into an empty spiritual landscape but into a politically organized trading community (Rimbert, *Vita Anskarii*).

The text must be read as Christian hagiography, but the institutional background is credible enough to reveal how missionary religion moved through royal and commercial networks.

## 19.3 Merchants as carriers of religion

Long-distance traders carried beliefs along with goods. Christian merchants could require burial or worship; Scandinavian travellers encountered churches abroad; crosses and Christian objects circulated before mass conversion. Towns therefore accelerated religious diffusion because they condensed interregional contact (Corsi 2020).

## 19.4 Pagan ritual and the town

Pre-Christian cult may have been associated with nearby elite centres and assembly places rather than monumental temples inside every town. Kaupang’s relationship to Skiringssal is especially important because the commercial settlement existed within a landscape of elite and cultic significance. The city should therefore be understood as embedded in a **ritual hinterland**, not as a secular bubble (Skre 2007).

Amulets, miniature objects, burial customs, and ritual deposition provide more direct evidence than speculative identifications of individual buildings as temples.

## 19.5 Christianity and the later urban transformation

During the tenth and eleventh centuries, churches increasingly became permanent urban institutions. Christianity supported royal administration, literacy, cemetery organization, ecclesiastical taxation, and connections to continental Europe. Corsi argues that political consolidation, economic development, and Christianization together shaped the later Danish urban system (Corsi 2020).

Urban churches also altered space. Processions, bells, burial grounds, saints’ cults, and ecclesiastical property generated new movement patterns and identities. Evidence from early medieval Odense shows how church and king used urban space to stage authority and embed Christian practice in everyday life (Haase and Bjerregaard 2022).

# 20. Sanitation, waste, smoke, mud, parasites, and health

## 20.1 Viking cities were biologically intense environments

Dense occupation concentrates human waste, animal dung, slaughter refuse, food scraps, ash, craft waste, standing water, smoke, insects, and pathogens. The celebrated preservation of York and Dublin exists partly because enormous quantities of wet organic debris accumulated beneath later streets and buildings (Addyman 1989; Reilly 2024).

A romantic reconstructed street of clean timber fronts therefore misses one of the strongest archaeological signals of urban life: **waste was everywhere, and managing it was continual work**.

## 20.2 York: an “organic world”

P. V. Addyman’s classic synthesis of York’s environmental archaeology concluded that conditions in the Viking Age reached an historical low point in the city, describing an environment built upon rotting organic material. Water supply, housing, rubbish, and effluent disposal all contributed to public-health conditions substantially worse than later improvements (Addyman 1989).

This does not mean every person lived ankle-deep in sewage at all times. It means disposal systems were insufficient to prevent organic accumulation in a dense wet settlement.

## 20.3 Dublin: insects as witnesses

Eileen Reilly’s 2024 study of Fishamble Street reconstructs living conditions through insects recovered from house floors, plots, streets, and surrounding deposits. Different insect ecologies distinguish decaying plant material, dung, stored products, dampness, and other microhabitats. The result is a fine-grained history of how urban dirt was produced and managed rather than a simplistic verdict of “filthy” or “clean” (Reilly 2024).

The key insight is that hygiene was **practice**, not a binary state. People swept, resurfaced, rebuilt, dumped, moved waste, and tried to control living surfaces, yet the city continually generated more organic material.

## 20.4 Human waste

Latrines, pits, midden deposits, and diffuse waste all occurred in early medieval urban contexts. Where formal latrines are absent, chamber vessels, yard disposal, manure heaps, and removal outside dense areas are possible. Archaeology rarely provides one universal sanitation method.

Parasite eggs from cess and intestinal contexts in northern European towns demonstrate that worms and fecal contamination were ordinary public-health burdens. Clean drinking water was difficult to protect where wells, animals, and waste occupied cramped space.

## 20.5 Smoke and indoor air

Open hearths provided heat, light, and cooking. Without modern chimneys, smoke escaped through doors, roof openings, and permeable building fabric. Indoor surfaces would accumulate soot, and respiratory irritation was likely. Craft processes added fumes: smithing, casting, dyeing, hide processing, and fuel combustion.

## 20.6 Fire

Wood, thatch, textiles, stored fuel, open hearths, furnaces, and close-packed houses made fire an omnipresent hazard. A workshop spark could threaten neighbours. Fire archaeology at many early towns reflects repeated destruction and rebuilding. Urban regulation of hearths or hazardous crafts is plausible, but direct early law is sparse.

## 20.7 Mud and footwear

Organic streets and high water tables made footwear essential. The enormous leather assemblages from York and Dublin are not merely fashion archives; they reflect the wear imposed by dirty, abrasive, wet surfaces. Constant shoe repair would have been part of urban maintenance.

## 20.8 Disease and mortality

Crowding increases transmission of respiratory and gastrointestinal disease, while contaminated water and parasites create chronic stress. Skeletal populations from urban cemeteries can document trauma, infection, nutritional stress, and dental disease, but cemetery samples are socially and chronologically selective. Mean age at death among excavated adults must never be confused with life expectancy at birth.

## 20.9 Smellscape

A Viking city smelled of woodsmoke, hearth food, animals, dung, wet timber, fish, hides, metalworking, sweat, stale ale, river mud, slaughter, latrine waste, and occasionally imported aromatics or tar. Smell varied block by block. A waterfront in fish season, a metalworking plot, and a textile household were different sensory worlds.

The smellscape is partly inferential, but the materials producing those smells are archaeologically documented. A truly accurate reconstruction should therefore be **sensory and uneven**, not uniformly foul and not sanitized.

# 21. Security, defence, violence, fire, and political instability

## 21.1 Wealth attracted danger

Towns concentrated silver, imported goods, ships, and people who could be ransomed. They were obvious targets. Fortifications at Birka and Hedeby, defended Dublin, and York’s inherited walls all demonstrate that commerce required security (Price et al. 2019; UNESCO Hedeby-Danevirke; Wallace 2016).

## 21.2 Defence could be urban and regional

A rampart was only one layer. Lookouts, naval forces, royal retainers, nearby hillforts, controlled channels, and political alliances also protected towns. Hedeby’s relation to the Danevirke is the clearest example of urban defence embedded in a larger strategic landscape.

## 21.3 Birka’s crisis in Rimbert

Rimbert describes Birka threatened while its king was absent. Merchants and inhabitants confront the possibility of attack, ransom the city with silver, and seek religious aid. Whatever hagiographic shaping is present, the episode captures the economic logic of urban vulnerability: concentrated wealth could buy peace precisely because attackers knew the town possessed it (Rimbert, *Vita Anskarii*).

## 21.4 Warriors in town

Warriors were present, but Viking cities should not be imagined as military camps. Hadley and ten Harkel’s volume deliberately asks what happens to “warrior” identity in urban settings, where civic, occupational, household, ethnic, and class identities could compete with martial display. Weapons and warrior graves remain part of the evidence, but everyday city life was sustained by far more than fighters (Hadley and ten Harkel eds. 2013).

## 21.5 Crime

Theft, fraud, assault, and property damage are predictable in any dense settlement. Direct crime statistics do not exist. Legal jurisdiction, locks, keys, chests, fences, and guarded wealth reveal the problem indirectly. A market where strangers exchanged silver had particular need for trusted measures and enforceable norms.

## 21.6 Political regime change

York and Dublin repeatedly changed rulers. Hedeby lay in a contested frontier. Urban households therefore had to survive dynastic change without necessarily abandoning everyday craft and trade. Political instability could disrupt networks, but successful rulers also had reason to preserve a profitable city rather than destroy its economic base.

# 22. Death, cemeteries, and urban belonging

## 22.1 Cities needed places for their dead

Permanent settlement creates permanent cemeteries. Burial grounds therefore provide one of the strongest checks on claims of seasonality. Ribe’s early burial evidence, Birka’s vast cemetery complex, and later Christian urban graveyards all demonstrate communities whose relationship to place extended beyond a market visit (Croix et al. 2020; Price et al. 2019).

## 22.2 Birka’s social archive

Birka’s roughly 3,000 visible mound burials, with around 1,100 excavated historically, constitute an extraordinary social archive. Burial forms range widely in wealth and practice. Chamber graves, weapon burials, jewellery-rich graves, children’s graves, cremations, and inhumations reveal a socially differentiated community with multiple cultural connections (Price et al. 2019; Ambrosiani).

## 22.3 Burial does not equal biography

A person buried with a “foreign” object was not necessarily foreign-born; a person born elsewhere could be buried in local style. Isotope research at Ribe makes this mismatch explicit. Burial is a social performance by survivors as much as a record of the deceased’s origin (Croix et al. 2020).

## 22.4 Christian cemeteries and urban change

As Christianity spread, burial increasingly clustered around churches and followed new ritual norms. Cemetery location therefore became part of urban planning and ecclesiastical power. The transition is especially visible in later Viking/early medieval towns such as Sigtuna and Danish royal centres (Corsi 2020; Krzewińska et al. 2018).

# 23. Comparative city dossiers

## 23.1 Ribe: the early North Sea city

### Foundation and role
Ribe emerged around **700 CE** on Denmark’s western coast and is currently the earliest Scandinavian settlement widely accepted as urban. Its riverine access connected it to the North Sea while an inland road connected it to Jutland. Rather than a random market sprawl, its early settlement was organized around a principal street and plots (Croix 2015; Sindbæk ed. 2022).

### Economy
Ribe specialized in craft and exchange from an early date. Excavations document non-ferrous metalworking, ironworking, glass-bead production, amber working, bone and antler craft, carpentry, textile production, imported ceramics and glass, and early coin use. The Northern Emporium project recovered more than 100,000 artefacts and reconstructed networks reaching Western Europe, Scandinavia, the Baltic/east, the Middle East, and Indian Ocean-linked circulation (Sindbæk eds. 2022, 2023).

### Households
High-resolution excavation shows domestic and craft activity occupying the same plots. One house phase emphasized food preparation, storage, sitting/sleeping, and weaving; later activity incorporated metalworking. This is prime evidence for the household-workshop model.

### Population
Strontium analysis suggests many early buried inhabitants were local or regional, while some were non-local and cultural affiliations were wider than birthplace. Ribe was cosmopolitan through connections as much as through mass immigration (Croix et al. 2020).

### Political significance
Early coinage, demarcation, later fortification, and its position within Danish political development show increasing elite interest. Exact property law remains unknown. Ribe is best seen as an early network city that became progressively more institutionalized.

### What it felt like
A resident lived amid craft noise, smoke, imported objects, muddy movement surfaces, ship traffic, livestock products, and a steady flow of visitors. The town was small enough that recurring households mattered but connected enough that an object or visitor might come from hundreds or thousands of kilometres away.

## 23.2 Hedeby: the transshipment powerhouse

### Strategic geography
Hedeby occupied the Schlei inlet near the base of Jutland, close to an overland transfer route between Baltic and North Sea systems. Its value came from geography amplified by political intervention. It became one of the Viking world’s most important trading towns (Kalmring 2024; UNESCO Hedeby-Danevirke).

### Planned space
Shoreline settlement was divided into plots and paths. Some boundaries were renewed in the same location for generations. A major shore-parallel path appears sufficiently regular to imply higher-level maintenance. Plot divisions extended toward landing stages, suggesting a close relationship between private or household commercial space and waterfront access (Wichmann et al. 2025).

### Economy
Hedeby hosted metalworking, craft, imported goods, coinage, ship traffic, and large-scale transfer. It linked Scandinavian, Frankish, Frisian, Slavic, Baltic, and wider networks. Its location made it a natural place to reload cargo between routes.

### Rule
Hedeby is the strongest case for the interaction of royal power and commerce. The Godfred/Reric tradition, Danevirke, minting, strategic location, and later textual/legal comparison all indicate that rulers treated it as more than a spontaneous market.

### Population
Cemeteries and artefacts indicate varied origin and status. Some merchants and craftspeople were resident; others were seasonal or transient. Foreign presence was economically necessary.

### End
Hedeby’s decline and the growth of nearby Schleswig show that urban functions can migrate. The network survived even when the old town did not.

## 23.3 Birka: Mälaren gateway and royal trading town

### Scale and form
Founded around 750, Birka may have supported approximately **700–1,000 residents** for roughly two centuries. Its lakefront settlement, rampart, hillfort, Black Earth deposits, and extensive cemeteries make it one of the best-defined early Viking cities (Price et al. 2019).

### Political institutions
Rimbert places kings, a local prefect, merchants, and collective assembly in the town. This is unusually direct written evidence for the political anatomy of an emporium.

### Economy
Birka was a hub for metalwork, textiles, imported luxury goods, eastern silver, and Baltic exchange. Its role cannot be reduced to exotica: animal studies show large provisioning flows, including cattle potentially brought from more than 180 km away (Schjerven et al. 2024).

### Society
Thousands of graves reveal marked social difference and cultural diversity. The city included wealthy households, children, women with elaborate jewellery, people buried with weapons, and individuals participating in far-flung material traditions.

### Daily life
Birka’s town population depended heavily on its Mälaren hinterland. Pigs may have been kept locally, while cattle and sheep products came through wider networks. Workshops, animal processing, harbour traffic, and burial landscapes placed subsistence, craft, trade, and identity in close proximity.

## 23.4 Kaupang: compact Norwegian commercial town

### Scale
Kaupang developed around 800 in Vestfold, near the elite centre of Skiringssal. It was compact, divided into perhaps **90–100 central plots** commonly around **40–80 m²**, and may have contained a few hundred permanent residents at peak (Skre ed. 2007; McGraw and Mjærum 2024).

### Settlement trajectory
An early more seasonal phase was followed by permanent buildings and sustained plot occupation. This makes Kaupang a textbook case for observing market-to-town development.

### Economy
Kaupang produced one of the richest assemblages of weights, balances, coins, hacksilver, and imported objects in Norway. The silver evidence shows a shift from relatively limited early ninth-century bullion use toward increased silver transactions after the influx of Islamic dirhams and standardized weights later in the century (Skre ed. 2008).

### People
Artefacts indicate wide connections, including Frisian and Slavic links. One household has been interpreted as a Frisian merchant context. The town’s material diversity far exceeds ordinary rural settlement (Skre ed. 2011).

### Political context
Its relationship with Skiringssal and elite power makes royal or aristocratic involvement likely. Regular plots may indicate superior ownership or site-level regulation, but exact tenure law is not preserved.

## 23.5 Jorvik/York: an Anglo-Scandinavian manufacturing city

### Urban inheritance
York differed from Scandinavian emporia because Vikings took control of a place with Roman fortifications and an Anglian urban history. The Scandinavian city therefore inherited streets, walls, ecclesiastical landscapes, and regional political importance rather than beginning on an empty shore (Palliser 2014; Addyman 1989; Walton Rogers 1997).

### Coppergate
Excavation at 16–22 Coppergate produced deep waterlogged deposits and tens of thousands of artefacts. Preservation reveals wood, leather, textiles, plant remains, insects, tools, houses, and manufacturing waste. The archaeology is a near-microscopic view of urban craft life.

### Textiles
More than a thousand textile-production artefacts represent fibre preparation, spinning, weaving, dyeing, finishing, cutting, and sewing. In the Anglo-Scandinavian phase, textile work remained broadly distributed across tenements before later specialization (Walton Rogers 1997).

### Environment
York’s organic accumulation created severe sanitation problems. Addyman’s public-health synthesis describes Viking-age conditions as an historical low in the city’s long environmental sequence (Addyman 1989).

### Society
York was not simply a Scandinavian colonial town. Its culture fused Scandinavian and English practices while remaining a major royal, ecclesiastical, manufacturing, and trading centre.

## 23.6 Dublin: the dense Hiberno-Scandinavian city

### Origins
A defended Viking ship camp was established on or near the Liffey in the ninth century. After renewed Viking control from 917, Dublin grew into a dense port and kingdom centre with organized plots, defences, craft, and long-distance trade (Wallace 2016; Reilly 2024).

### Urban plots
Fishamble Street preserves long narrow tenements defined by wattle fences. Replanning could subdivide larger properties into smaller plots; pathways and ancillary buildings were integral to each unit. Boundaries sometimes persisted across many rebuilding phases (Simpson 1998; Reilly 2024).

### Houses
The common Type 1 house was rectangular and low, with opposing doors, a broad central strip, raised side benches/beds, and a central hearth. Its architecture fit the narrow urban plot exceptionally well (Wallace 2016).

### Crafts
Dublin preserves evidence for textile work, leatherworking, ironworking, bone and antler craft, amber, woodwork, carpentry, ship-related activity, and metalworking. Its river and sea access made it a major Atlantic node.

### Living conditions
Reilly’s insect study reconstructs floors, waste, dampness, dung, decaying matter, and hygiene practices in extraordinary detail. Dublin is consequently one of the best sites for reconstructing the *feel* of Viking city life rather than just its trade.

### Slavery and politics
Dublin was deeply entangled in raiding, warfare, and slave trafficking. It was also a royal centre whose political history connected Ireland, Britain, and the Scandinavian Atlantic.

## 23.7 Sigtuna: migration and the royal-Christian town

Founded in the late tenth century, Sigtuna represents a newer urban model associated more closely with kingship and Christianity. Its cemeteries and settlement document a population with striking genetic and geographic diversity. The ancient-DNA and strontium study demonstrates local, regional, and long-distance immigrants, with male and female mobility both important (Krzewińska et al. 2018).

Sigtuna therefore shows urbanization moving beyond the classic emporium. Political administration, Christian institutions, minting, and migration increasingly converged.

## 23.8 Truso: Baltic port of exchange

Truso, near modern Elbląg at Janów Pomorski, was a major ninth-century port of trade at the Baltic-Slavic frontier. Archaeology identifies Scandinavian-linked merchants and craftspeople, including connections to Denmark and Gotland, alongside local and regional populations. Silver, craft debris, and imported goods place it firmly within Baltic exchange (Adamczyk 2019; Metslang 2004).

Truso is important because “Viking city” need not mean a town politically inside Scandinavia. Viking urban networks crossed cultural frontiers and could operate in mixed settlements.

## 23.9 Wolin: Slavic city with a Scandinavian presence

Wolin on the southern Baltic became a major urban and commercial centre with extensive contacts. Scandinavian merchants and warriors are archaeologically visible especially from the later tenth century, but the town was not simply a “Viking colony.” It was a Slavic urban centre embedded in shared Baltic networks (Adamczyk 2019).

Its silver record demonstrates how payment and prestige overlapped. Wolin also illustrates why ethnic labels must not replace economic analysis: Norse participants could be influential without constituting the whole city.

## 23.10 Staraya Ladoga: river gateway to the east

Staraya Ladoga stood on the Volkhov, accessible from the Baltic through the Gulf of Finland, Neva, and Lake Ladoga. Scandinavian traders, craftspeople, and settlers were attracted to the site from the eighth century onward, but Ladoga was a multiethnic riverine community rather than an exclusively Scandinavian town (Grigorjeva 2022).

Its strategic role was directional: it channelled Baltic traffic into the river systems leading toward the interior of the Rus world. Craft, weights, silver, and imported goods make Ladoga a crucial eastern counterpart to North Sea emporia.

# 24. An evidence-based day in a Viking city

The following reconstruction is **synthetic, not a claim about one excavated individual**. Every major activity is grounded in evidence from one or more Viking towns, but no single household necessarily performed all of them.

### Before sunrise

A resident wakes on a raised side bench or bedding area inside a timber, wattle, turf, or plank-built house. The hearth’s embers are revived. Smoke gathers before finding its way through roof and doorway. Someone fetches water. If the household keeps a pig or poultry, animals are already audible. In a wet town the lane outside may be muddy, uneven, and layered over older road surfaces (Wallace 2016; Reilly 2024; Addyman 1989).

### Early morning

Food is prepared from stored grain, dairy products, yesterday’s meat or fish, or other supplies. A spindle may already be turning. A household member checks tools and material left from the previous day. In a metalworking plot, charcoal and furnace equipment are prepared; in a combmaker’s yard, antler blanks and offcuts accumulate; in a merchant household, scales and weights are accessible because someone may arrive to bargain (Ashby and Sindbæk eds. 2020; Sindbæk ed. 2022).

### The harbour comes alive

A ship has arrived or is expected. Crew carry news before cargo has even been unloaded. Porters and household assistants move barrels, sacks, stone, timber, hides, or small high-value packages. If animals are aboard or have been driven overland, they must be controlled. Officials or local powerholders may claim dues. Commercial partners identify one another (Kalmring 2024; Sindbæk 2007a).

### Midmorning craft

Work spreads across houses and yards. Hammering, sawing, grinding, spindle motion, loom noise, hide cutting, and woodworking produce an uneven urban soundscape. Smoke from a forge drifts toward a neighbour. A child watches, carries scraps, or plays nearby. Customers do not enter a “shopping district” separated from residence; they approach the same plots where people eat and sleep.

### Market exchange

A farmer or supplier arrives with food, wool, hides, fuel, animals, or raw material. A craft household needs those necessities because its own plot cannot feed it. Payment may be coin, weighed silver, goods, or a transaction embedded in an existing relationship. A balance settles the quantity of silver but not necessarily the price (Skre ed. 2008; Graham-Campbell ed. 2018).

### Noon and information

A meal creates a break but also an exchange of information. Who has arrived from Hedeby? Is a route dangerous? What are cattle fetching? Has a ruler died? Is silver plentiful? Are ships preparing to leave? Information is economically valuable, and a node where many routes cross produces it continuously (Sindbæk 2007a, 2023).

### Afternoon household production

Textile work continues because fibre production consumes enormous time. Repairs are made. Shoes, clothing, containers, and tools are maintained rather than discarded casually. A specialist may perform a technically demanding stage while other household members do repetitive preparation. Waste is swept or pushed outward. Some of it enters a midden; some becomes part of the street deposit (Walton Rogers 1997; Reilly 2024).

### Assembly, dispute, or official business

Not every day includes a formal assembly, but disputes need forums. A disagreement over payment, theft, debt, injury, or rights may reach a local official or thing. Foreign merchants need rules that do not depend entirely on kinship. Royal protection is meaningful only if someone can enforce it (Rimbert; Kalmring 2024).

### Evening

The urban population contracts into households and social gatherings. Ale, food, gaming, music, story, and conversation reinforce relationships. Visiting sailors may be hosted. A merchant may negotiate tomorrow’s transaction after the formal market activity has ended. Children sleep while adults continue work in the light of fire or lamps.

### Night

Fire remains a threat. So does theft. Goods are secured in chests, bags, or locked spaces. Animals and human waste continue to shape the environment. Beyond the rampart or shoreline, the town is quiet enough that a watch may hear approaching boats. Tomorrow the same urban system must be fed again.

The crucial point is not picturesque detail. It is that **every hour joins household life to an exchange network**. Eating depends on rural supply. Craft depends on materials. Trade depends on trust and authority. Shipping depends on labour. Security depends on politics. Urbanity is the simultaneous operation of all of them.

# 25. Direct answers to the central questions

## What was it like living in a Viking city?

It was **denser, noisier, more commercially connected, more socially diverse, and more dependent on strangers** than life on most farmsteads. Houses sat inside bounded plots close to neighbours. Craft could occur where people slept and ate. Streets were repeatedly resurfaced because mud and organic debris accumulated. Smoke, livestock, food waste, industry, river water, ships, and human waste created strong smells. Imported objects and unfamiliar languages were ordinary in major ports. Food had to be purchased or exchanged for because urban households lacked enough land to feed themselves. Fire and disease were serious hazards. At the same time, the city provided unprecedented access to goods, skills, information, patrons, social partners, and economic opportunity (Hadley and ten Harkel eds. 2013; Reilly 2024; Addyman 1989; Sindbæk eds. 2022–2023).

## What did people actually do all day?

They cooked, fetched water, maintained fires, spun, wove, stitched, carved, hammered, cast metal, cut leather, repaired shoes, worked wood, made combs and beads, loaded ships, handled animals, carried cargo, weighed silver, bargained, stored goods, repaired buildings, cleaned floors, removed or displaced waste, watched children, socialized, hosted travellers, attended assemblies, guarded property, worshipped, and prepared for the next trading or sailing opportunity. A single household could combine several of these activities.

## What were the “jobs”?

The best-supported urban roles include merchants, sailors, shipmasters, shipwrights, blacksmiths, non-ferrous metalworkers, gold- and silversmiths, moneyers, beadmakers, combmakers, bone/antler workers, carpenters, turners, coopers, leatherworkers, shoemakers, textile workers, dyers, tailors, food processors, animal handlers, porters, builders, officials, guards, retainers, clergy, servants, and enslaved labourers. However, many were **activities within a household economy rather than exclusive full-time occupations** (Ashby and Sindbæk eds. 2020; Walton Rogers 1997; Sindbæk eds. 2022–2023).

## How did the economy function?

The city bought food and raw material from a hinterland, transformed some of those materials through craft, imported commodities and silver from long-distance routes, and redistributed finished products, prestige goods, services, and information. Money could take the form of coins, weighed silver, hacksilver, or standardized commodities. Rulers protected and taxed exchange. The city survived only while ships and rural suppliers continued to choose it as a node (Skre ed. 2008; Graham-Campbell ed. 2018; Kalmring 2024).

## Did they use barter?

Yes, but **barter is not an adequate name for the economic system**. Direct good-for-good exchange coexisted with silver by weight, coins, commodity money, gifts, obligations, and negotiated transactions. By the ninth century, some towns show sophisticated weighing systems and large quantities of transactional silver (Skre ed. 2008).

## Who supplied food?

Farmers, herders, fishers, and producers in the surrounding region, supplemented by longer-distance provisioning. Birka provides direct isotopic evidence that some cattle travelled more than 180 km to the town. Urban households might keep some pigs or poultry, but no major emporium was self-sufficient (Schjerven et al. 2024).

## How did someone move to a Viking city?

There was no universal immigration office. People probably entered through household and kin networks, marriage, patronage, merchant partnerships, craft opportunities, royal service, religious service, slavery, or the transfer/reallocation of an existing plot. Biological evidence from Ribe and Sigtuna proves that urban populations included migrants, but it cannot tell us the legal procedure by which every person took up residence (Croix et al. 2020; Krzewińska et al. 2018).

## Could foreigners live there?

Clearly yes. Major Viking towns were cosmopolitan. Archaeology, texts, isotopes, ancient DNA, and imported artefacts all show non-local people and extensive foreign contact. The degree of permanent residence varied. Foreign merchants could also be politically controlled precisely because rulers wanted their trade (Kalmring 2024; Skre ed. 2011).

## Who owned a house?

An occupying household controlled and invested in its dwelling, but the legal relation between building and ground is rarely preserved. We should distinguish **house ownership/possession** from **land tenure**. It is plausible that a householder controlled the structure while occupying land ultimately claimed by a ruler or elite, but that model cannot be universalized.

## Who owned the land?

At some planned towns, royal or elite superior ownership is plausible. Kaupang’s regular plots have been interpreted in that direction; its association with Skiringssal strengthens the possibility. Yet no document proves that every Viking urban plot belonged to the king. The strongest statement is that **urban ground was regulated and allocated**, often within a political sphere controlled by powerful patrons (Molaug 2009; Skre ed. 2007; Kalmring 2024).

## Could a household inherit or transfer a plot?

Probably in some form, but direct proof is limited. Boundary continuity across generations shows continuity of the *plot*, not necessarily the bloodline. Inheritance, household succession, patronal reallocation, and negotiated transfer are all plausible mechanisms.

## Who ruled a town?

Usually a king, local ruler, or elite power stood above it, but daily rule could involve a prefect/reeve, officials, guards, moneyers, and an assembly. Birka provides unusually direct textual evidence for a king, a local prefect, merchants, and collective deliberation (Rimbert). York and Dublin could function as royal capitals in their own right.

## Did towns have their own laws?

There is strong evidence and argument for **special urban jurisdiction**, but the wording of early laws is mostly lost. Kalmring argues that major Scandinavian emporia were special legal-economic zones with separate jurisdiction and customs supervision. Later town law cannot simply be copied backward, but it preserves institutional developments with possible Viking roots (Kalmring 2024).

## Were there shops?

There were places where goods were sold, but the modern shopfront should not be assumed. A household plot could be workshop, residence, storehouse, and sales point at once. Market exchange could also occur in public spaces or harbours.

## Were there warehouses?

Storage was essential, but purpose-built warehouses are unevenly documented. Cargo could be stored in houses, outbuildings, chests, barrels, sheds, and waterfront structures. The archaeological absence of a monumental warehouse district does not imply just-in-time trade.

## Were there taverns and inns?

Hospitality, ale drinking, and lodging are certain; a standardized commercial inn or tavern institution is not securely demonstrated for every Viking town. Production writing should depict hosting and drinking confidently, but label a purpose-built “tavern” more cautiously unless tied to a particular source.

## Did towns have police?

Not in a modern institutional sense. Royal or local officials, guards, retainers, household defence, assemblies, and communal obligations provided security and enforcement. A “town watch” may be plausible in specific contexts but should not be universalized without evidence.

## Did they have taxes?

Yes in the broad sense of dues and customs associated with rulers and protected commerce. The exact rate and collection method are rarely preserved for the early period. Coinage, customs boundaries, royal officials, and comparative evidence make fiscal extraction one of the strongest reasons rulers invested in towns (Kalmring 2024).

## Were Viking towns rich?

They concentrated valuable goods, but not every resident was wealthy. Cemeteries and housing show strong social differences. A town could contain affluent merchants and metalworkers beside servants, dependants, labourers, children, captives, and poorer households. Urban wealth is a **distribution**, not an average.

## How many people lived there?

Numbers varied greatly and estimates are uncertain. Birka is often estimated at about 700–1,000; Kaupang perhaps several hundred; later York and Dublin were substantially larger urban systems. Population also fluctuated seasonally. Exact figures should be presented as ranges, not censuses.

## Were women economically important?

Yes. Textile production alone makes that unavoidable. Women also managed households, stored resources, migrated, participated in exchange, and transmitted skills. The extent of women’s long-distance merchant activity cannot be quantified, but the economy was not male-only (Walton Rogers 1997; Krzewińska et al. 2018).

## What did children do?

They played, learned, carried out age-appropriate household labour, and absorbed adult skills. Dublin preserves evidence of a distinct material childhood. Birka’s furnished child graves show that children carried household status and social identities (McAlister 2013; Swedish History Museum).

## Were enslaved people part of city life?

Yes. Viking raiding and trade moved captives through urban nodes, and restraints from towns support the historical evidence. Their ordinary household labour is harder to identify archaeologically because legal status does not leave a unique toolkit (Raffield 2019).

## Was urban life healthier than farm life?

Not necessarily. Dense population, contaminated water, smoke, parasites, waste, animals, and infectious disease created serious risks. Viking York in particular has been assessed as environmentally unhealthy. Towns offered economic advantages, not modern public-health advantages (Addyman 1989; Reilly 2024).

# 26. Major uncertainties and research cautions

## 26.1 Do not equate artefact with ethnicity

A Frisian pot does not prove a Frisian occupant; a Scandinavian brooch does not prove Scandinavian birthplace. Objects move through exchange, gifts, imitation, inheritance, and fashion. Isotope research repeatedly shows that cultural affiliation and geographic origin can diverge (Croix et al. 2020).

## 26.2 Do not equate burial role with everyday profession

A weapon burial does not prove a full-time soldier, and textile tools do not prove a full-time professional weaver. Grave goods are statements made by survivors as well as possessions.

## 26.3 Do not infer full-time specialization from one tool

A spindle whorl proves textile activity, not occupational exclusivity. A balance suggests weighing but not necessarily a career merchant. Production debris, spatial concentration, technical complexity, and repeated use strengthen occupational interpretation (Ashby and Sindbæk eds. 2020).

## 26.4 Do not project later guilds backward

Craft guilds are well documented later, but early Viking towns should not be populated with formal guild organizations unless specific evidence warrants it. Craft communities and knowledge networks can exist without chartered guilds.

## 26.5 Do not project later town law backward wholesale

Thirteenth-century Norwegian or Danish urban law can illuminate categories of problem but not prove ninth-century rules. Kalmring explicitly notes the scarcity of contemporary legal sources while arguing for earlier separate jurisdiction (Kalmring 2024).

## 26.6 Do not treat population estimates as census results

Urban density, occupied area, house size, cemetery count, and seasonal mobility produce wide uncertainty. Numbers such as Birka’s 700–1,000 are useful orders of magnitude, not exact totals.

## 26.7 Do not make every town Scandinavian-majority

Truso, Wolin, Dublin, York, and Ladoga were culturally mixed in different ways. “Viking urban network” is not the same as “ethnically homogeneous Scandinavian city.”

## 26.8 Do not make every town identical

An eighth-century Ribe craft plot, a Hedeby waterfront holding, a Birka household, an Anglo-Scandinavian York tenement, and a tenth-century Dublin plot belonged to related but distinct systems. Production design should preserve local architecture, street form, available materials, fortifications, harbour geography, and political context.

## 26.9 Do not sanitize the city

Wet organic streets, dung, slaughter, smoke, industrial waste, insects, and human effluent are strongly evidenced. Clean museum-reconstruction streets can mislead if reproduced without accumulated debris and daily maintenance.

## 26.10 Do not make the city uniformly filthy

The opposite stereotype is also wrong. People cleaned floors, renewed surfaces, dumped waste, rebuilt houses, and selected activity zones. Reilly’s Dublin work is valuable precisely because it reconstructs changing practices rather than a timeless sewer (Reilly 2024).

## 26.11 Do not portray every resident as wealthy

Imported goods are disproportionately visible and attractive to scholarship. Everyday wooden objects, repaired shoes, reused containers, common textiles, and food waste reveal ordinary households. Wealth inequality was substantial.

## 26.12 Do not forget seasonality

A harbour in summer sailing season could be crowded and multilingual; winter could be quieter, with more repair and household production. One “population” figure cannot capture that pulse.

# 27. Conclusion

The Viking city was one of the great institutional inventions of the Viking Age. It did not replace the farmstead, kin group, hall, thing, or ship. It **connected them at higher frequency**. By concentrating craftspeople, merchants, households, officials, strangers, and transport infrastructure, towns created environments in which exchange could occur repeatedly between people who were not close kin. That change required new forms of trust, measurement, legal protection, spatial regulation, and political supervision.

Archaeology now shows the city from the bottom up. It was a world of small plots and repeated fences; houses that were simultaneously homes and workshops; benches that became beds at night; hearths used for cooking beside craft processes; streets continually raised by organic accumulation; shoes worn down by wet lanes; imported glass and silver beside animal bones and dung; children learning inside workplaces; women producing the textiles on which households and ships depended; merchants weighing metal; cattle walking enormous distances toward slaughter; ships bringing both cargo and news; rulers attempting to convert movement into revenue; and households whose claim to a few dozen square metres might persist long after the original building decayed.

Economically, the city was **dependent rather than self-sufficient**. Its power came from specialization, and specialization required reliable external supply. A smith who spent the day making tools could not spend the same day ploughing enough land to feed a family. A beadmaker depended on glass and fuel. A merchant depended on ship crews and food sellers. A harbour depended on carpenters, porters, rope, and repair. Every specialist therefore generated demand for someone else’s labour. Urbanization was the multiplication of those dependencies inside a compact space.

Politically, towns gave rulers something rural society did not: a place where valuable movement could be **made visible**. Ships arrived at known waterfronts. Foreigners congregated in delimited zones. Silver passed over scales. Market disputes occurred before witnesses. A reeve or prefect could stand between king and merchant. Customs could be demanded. Coin could be minted. Fortifications could protect a concentrated asset. This explains why royal power so often hovers around Viking urban origins without requiring us to imagine that kings personally designed every lane.

Socially, the city weakened neither kinship nor status, but it multiplied alternative relationships. Neighbour, partner, client, shipmate, craft colleague, patron, creditor, customer, fellow believer, migrant, and official became more important categories of daily encounter. Towns were therefore engines of cultural recombination. Technologies, styles, languages, religious ideas, and identities met not once on a raid but repeatedly in the same streets.

The most defensible answer to the property question follows the same logic. Viking towns did not possess modern real-estate systems, but their ground was not a free-for-all. Planned plots, repeated boundaries, maintained streets, waterfront allocations, and political patronage demonstrate **regulated tenure**. Households possessed durable rights strong enough to justify investment. Rulers or elites probably exercised superior authority over land in at least some foundations. Yet the exact mixture of allocation, rent, inheritance, purchase, patronage, and succession remains beyond direct recovery for most early towns.

The Viking city should consequently be visualized neither as a rough camp of warriors nor as a picturesque row of clean craft shops. It was a **living economic machine made of households**: crowded, skilled, dirty, colourful, hierarchical, mobile, dangerous, innovative, and connected to a geographical world vastly larger than its physical footprint.

| Enslaved worker | Texts + restraints + trade context | Shackles/restraints sometimes; ordinary tools otherwise | Legal status usually invisible archaeologically |
| Child helper/learner | Dublin, household archaeology | Toys, miniatures, child graves | Labour roles rarely directly identifiable |

# Appendix C: Evidence-confidence scale

### A — Secure
Use in production and historical prose without special qualification.

Examples: Ribe had organized plots and intensive craft production; Kaupang used small plots; Birka had merchants and a local official in Rimbert; Dublin had repeated fenced tenements; York had extensive textile production; towns used scales and weights; women performed textile work; urban populations included migrants.

### B — Strongly supported inference
Use confidently but with words such as “likely,” “probably,” or “appears.”

Examples: higher authority maintained some major urban streets; royal/elite authorities controlled access to urban ground; particular waterfront facilities belonged to adjacent plot users; households marketed some of their own craft surplus.

### C — Plausible reconstruction
Good for a carefully labelled scene or interpretive passage, not as a bare fact.

Examples: a visiting merchant lodged with a commercial partner; an apprentice lived in a craft household; a plot was inherited by a child; a porter was paid in food or silver; neighbours organized fire response.

### D — Unsupported unless a site-specific source is found
Avoid as generic Viking-city fact.

Examples: elected mayors; standardized street addresses; universal property deeds; formal guild halls in eighth-century emporia; a professional police force; uniform zoning codes; a purpose-built tavern on every street; fixed opening hours; king owning every house; slavery identifiable by a special residential quarter.

# Research note on citation and evidentiary use

This paper intentionally relies most heavily on **excavation monographs, peer-reviewed archaeology, university-press syntheses, scientific isotope/aDNA studies, and primary texts**. Museum and heritage sources are used chiefly where they summarize excavated features or make specialist research accessible. Later medieval laws are treated as comparative material rather than direct transcripts of eighth- or ninth-century practice. Statements about tenure, specialised occupations, slavery, gender, or identity are calibrated to the evidentiary limits explained in Sections 2 and 26.

The most important recent works for production-level reconstruction are Kalmring’s *Towns and Commerce in Viking-Age Scandinavia* (2024), the two *Northern Emporium* volumes (2022–2023), Reilly’s *Dirt, Dwellings and Culture* (2024), Ashby and Sindbæk’s *Crafts and Social Networks in Viking Towns* (2020), Corsi’s *Urbanization in Viking Age and Medieval Denmark* (2020), the Kaupang publication series, Hadley and ten Harkel’s *Everyday Life in Viking-Age Towns* (2013), and the 2024 Birka provisioning study. Together they move Viking urban history away from a catalogue of exotic trade goods and toward a reconstruction of **systems, households, labour, food, law, and lived experience**.
