# 1. Georgia Free Camping Overview

Georgia has a large amount of free dispersed camping on the **Chattahoochee-Oconee National Forest**.

Dispersed camping is generally permitted throughout much of National Forest land unless a location is:

- Specifically closed
- Posted against camping
- Within an area with special restrictions
- A developed fee campground
- Temporarily closed because of fire, logging, road work, storms, or wildlife activity

Georgia DNR also recognizes primitive camping on numerous **National Forest Wildlife Management Areas (WMAs)**.

## General National Forest Stay Limit

Current Chattahoochee-Oconee rules generally limit camping to:

**14 total camping days within any rolling 30-day period across the National Forest.**

Moving to another campsite or forest road within the same National Forest does not automatically reset the limit.

After accumulating 14 days, remove your property and leave National Forest land before beginning another stay.

# 2. General Sanitation Rules

Where no toilet is available:

- Dig a cathole approximately **6-8 inches deep**
- Stay at least **200 feet from water**
- Stay at least **200 feet from trails**
- Stay at least **200 feet from campsites and shelters**
- Cover the cathole completely after use
- Never bury wipes
- Packing out toilet paper is preferable where practical
- Never dispose of human waste directly into streams, creeks, lakes, or drainage channels

A portable toilet plus sealed waste bags is preferable at heavily used camps.

---

# 3. Campfire Rules

Campfires are generally permitted when no temporary restriction is active.

Best practices:

- Use existing fire rings where available
- Keep fires small
- Burn only dead and down wood
- Never cut standing live or dead trees
- Keep water immediately available
- Fully drown and stir ashes
- Leave only when ashes are cold to the touch
- Check current Forest Service fire restrictions before every stay

Georgia can become very dry even outside midsummer.

---

# 4. Bear and Food Storage

Black bears occur throughout northern Georgia.

Store:

- Food
- Trash
- Toothpaste
- Soap
- Cooking grease
- Deodorant
- Other scented items

inside a locked vehicle or approved bear-resistant container.

## Appalachian Trail Special Rule

A bear canister is seasonally required on portions of the Appalachian Trail between **Jarrard Gap and Neel Gap**, generally from **March 1 through June 1**.

Special campfire restrictions may also apply along parts of this corridor.

# 17. Seasonal Strategy

## 🌱 Spring: March-May

Advantages:

- Pleasant temperatures
- Green forest
- Streams flowing strongly

Risks:

- Severe thunderstorms
- Tornadoes
- Hail
- Flooded creeks
- Muddy roads
- Washed-out crossings

### Best Strategy
Use central Georgia forests during unstable weather because evacuation and road access are easier.

Move into the mountains during stable dry periods.

---

## ☀️ Summer: June-August

### North Georgia Mountains
Advantages:

- Cooler than central Georgia
- Better sleeping temperatures
- Higher elevation

Typical hazards:

- High humidity
- Thunderstorms
- Mosquitoes
- Ticks
- Yellow jackets
- Poison ivy
- Falling limbs
- Flash flooding

### Best Summer Areas
- Padena
- Blue Ridge district
- Cohutta
- Swallow Creek
- Higher northern Georgia sites

### Less Ideal
- Cedar Creek
- Redlands
- Low-elevation central Georgia

---

## 🍂 Fall: September-November

Often one of Georgia's best camping periods.

Advantages:

- Lower humidity
- Less rain
- Comfortable temperatures
- Fewer insects

Risks:

- Hunting activity
- Leaf-season crowds
- Falling temperatures
- Early mountain ice

### Recommendation
Wear blaze orange around active hunting areas.

---

## ❄️ Winter: December-February

### North Georgia Mountains
Expect:

- Frequent freezing nights
- Occasional snow
- Ice
- Mud / freeze-thaw road damage

### Central Georgia
Generally:

- Warmer
- Less snow
- Easier roads
- Better winter nomad conditions

### Best Winter Areas
1. Redlands
2. Cedar Creek
3. Lake Russell during warmer spells
4. Mountains only during stable favorable forecasts

# 18. Major Hazards

## Flash Flooding
Avoid camping immediately beside:

- Creeks
- Drainage channels
- Low stream terraces

Heavy rain upstream can create flooding even if your campsite receives little rain.

---

## Black Bears
Secure all food and scented items.

Never leave food unattended outside the vehicle.

---

## Snakes
Potential species include:

- Copperheads
- Timber rattlesnakes

Watch:

- Rock piles
- Logs
- Creek margins
- Tall grass

---

## Ticks
Perform daily tick checks.

Treat clothing or gear appropriately when desired.

---

## Yellow Jackets
Most problematic in late summer and fall.

Avoid disturbing ground nests.

---

## Poison Ivy
Extremely common.

Learn identification in all growth stages.

---

## Falling Limbs
Avoid camping under:

- Dead trees
- Broken branches
- Storm-damaged trees

Risk increases following:

- High winds
- Saturated soil
- Severe thunderstorms

---

## Road Washouts
A Forest Service road that was easy last week may become difficult after a major storm.

Always have:

- Offline maps
- Traction boards
- Recovery strap
- Tire repair gear
- Air compressor
- Water
- Extra food
- Emergency battery power

---

## Hunting
Many WMAs are actively hunted.

During hunting season:

- Wear blaze orange
- Keep camp visible
- Avoid blocking roads
- Expect gunfire in legal hunting areas

---

# 19. Places Not Counted as Strictly Free

The following may appear in older "free camping" lists but should not be assumed free.

## Lake Conasauga Campground
Developed campground with a nightly fee.

Nearby dispersed camping may still be available on National Forest land.

## Sarah's Creek Developed Campground
May require reservation / fee depending on current management.

## Other Recreation.gov Campgrounds
Many developed Forest Service campgrounds now charge fees.

## Georgia State-Owned WMAs
Some require:

- Georgia Lands Pass
- Hunting license
- Fishing license
- Other qualifying access permit

National Forest WMAs are different and may not require the same access fee merely to enter.

# 24. Practical Cellular Strategy

Georgia mountain cellular service is extremely terrain-dependent.

A carrier showing "coverage" on a national map does not guarantee usable internet inside a mountain hollow.

## Best Practice

At each candidate campsite:

1. Park before unloading.
2. Test all available SIM / carrier options.
3. Check:
   - LTE / 5G signal
   - Ping
   - Download
   - Upload
4. Test again inside the vehicle.
5. Test with hotspot enabled.
6. Walk 50-100 yards toward a higher/open position.
7. Mark the exact campsite in offline maps.
8. Record which carrier worked.

A site with weak signal may improve dramatically with:

- Higher placement
- External antenna
- Directional antenna
- Window-mounted antenna
- Repeater used within legal limits

# 27. Final Nomad Takeaway

Georgia has three very different free-camping environments:

### Northern Mountains
Best for:

- Summer
- Solitude
- Scenic forest
- Cooler weather

Weaknesses:

- Rough roads
- Weak cellular service
- Expensive repeated supply runs

### Foothill / Dahlonega Belt
Best for:

- Balancing forest access with services
- Easier supply runs
- Moderate cellular access

Standout:

**Jake Mountain / Chestatee region**

### Oconee National Forest / Central Georgia
Best for:

- Winter
- Hyundai Accent access
- Cellular reliability
- Easy supply logistics
- Long-term slow-nomad living

Standouts:

**Cedar Creek and Redlands**
