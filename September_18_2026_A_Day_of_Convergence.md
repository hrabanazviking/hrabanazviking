# September 18, 2026 — A Day of Convergence

Today feels like one of those rare days when several things I have been thinking about for years suddenly came together into a much clearer picture.

I better defined who I am, how I want to live, what kind of stability actually works for me, how my spirituality fits into that life, and how several of my major AI goals may belong inside one larger focused project rather than remaining scattered ideas.

There is a strange sense that several separate roads all reached the same crossroads today.

## Stability Through Mobility

One of the biggest realizations is that I am not actually seeking instability by becoming a slow nomad.

I am engineering **a more stable life through geographic flexibility**.

Conventional fixed-location living has repeatedly created instability for me because changing locations is enormously expensive in money, energy, planning, paperwork, and emotional effort. When something about an environment becomes intolerable, I cannot simply leave. I have historically had to endure the situation until I accumulated enough resources to make a major move.

That has happened repeatedly throughout my life.

Kendallville was actually an important example because I stayed there for seven years, longer than I have normally stayed anywhere. The location worked extremely well for me for years. Most people there were live-and-let-live. They knew I was not Christian and simply did not care.

The problem began when one person moved next to me who, from my experience of her behavior, became intensely hostile toward me because I was not Christian. One nearby person was eventually enough to make an otherwise good location feel intolerable.

My current home has taught me something similar from another angle. The immediate environment is actually quite good. My current neighbors are usually quiet and do not bother me. The couple managing the park are friendly, accepting, and understand that I am an extremely private person, so they leave me alone.

The destabilizing factor came from the fact that John owns the building and could therefore use control over the property as leverage over my personal freedom.

That helped me understand something fundamental:

**A fixed address does not automatically create stability.**

For me, stability comes from retaining the freedom to change environments when necessary.

Every individual location is filled with things outside my control. I cannot control who moves nearby, what people do, what the weather does, what noises appear, what social dynamics develop, or how a particular place changes over time.

But I can control **whether I remain there**.

That distinction may become one of the central principles of my nomad life.

I cannot control a location.

I can control my location.

## The Thirteen-Day Rhythm

I also developed a much clearer practical rhythm for nomad life today.

My normal pattern can be approximately:

**Camp for 13 days.  
Travel roughly 300 miles.  
Camp for another 13 days.  
Repeat.**

The exact distance will naturally vary depending on geography, legal camping opportunities, weather, events, and travel corridors, but approximately 300 miles seems to be emerging naturally as the average distance between the major locations in my system.

This rhythm is practical because many public-land camping systems use approximately 14-day limits. Leaving on day 13 gives me a comfortable safety margin rather than repeatedly pushing right against a legal deadline.

But the numbers also have spiritual meaning.

**13 is a sacred number in witchcraft.**

**3 is a deeply sacred number throughout Pagan traditions**, and nine, which is especially important within Norse tradition, is three multiplied by three.

So completely unintentionally at first, my practical nomad architecture began developing a sacred numerical rhythm:

**13 days of dwelling.  
Approximately 300 miles of journey.**

The logistical and spiritual layers fit together.

I do not need to artificially force every trip to exactly 300 miles. The symbolism describes the rhythm rather than becoming another rigid rule.

## High Geographic Mobility at Low Subjective Speed

Another important realization is that this lifestyle does not mean constantly sitting in my car.

Quite the opposite.

If I stay somewhere for roughly thirteen days and then make one significant travel hop, most of my life is spent **camped**, not driving.

That means my actual experience can remain relaxed.

I can set up camp, spend time in nature, work online, interact with AI, develop software, write, exercise in VR, perform spiritual rituals, meditate, explore the surrounding area, and simply exist somewhere peaceful.

Then I move.

Then I settle again.

My car connects the places where I live my life rather than becoming the place where my entire life occurs.

That creates something I might describe as:

**high geographic mobility at low subjective speed.**

Over a year I could travel across an enormous portion of the United States while rarely feeling as though I am constantly traveling.

That is radically different from conventional road-trip culture.

## A Continental Slow-Nomad Network

I now have enough travel corridors that they are beginning to form a huge national loop.

There are southern winter routes, western routes, and now a northern summer corridor through South Dakota and Michigan that can also connect me back toward Angola, Indiana.

These corridors allow me to move enormous distances gradually while keeping my monthly travel budget remarkably consistent.

Instead of suddenly driving 1,000 or 1,500 miles because I randomly decided to go somewhere, I can move through known nodes separated by manageable distances and spend approximately two weeks at each one.

The major exception would be an urgent administrative or domicile-related task that required me to travel a large distance quickly. That could temporarily create a higher fuel month, but it would be an exception rather than the normal structure of my life.

My movement therefore becomes flexible without becoming financially chaotic.

I also realized that my travel direction usually does not need to be predetermined.

My strongest constraints are:

- seasonal weather,
- legal camping conditions,
- my normal travel budget,
- unusual events I want to attend,
- occasional administrative obligations,
- and whatever locations currently interest me.

Otherwise I can travel clockwise, counterclockwise, east, west, north, south, linger somewhere, revisit somewhere, or choose an entirely different branch of the network.

I am not building an itinerary.

I am building a **mobility web**.

## Seasonal Migration Instead of a Fixed Schedule

Weather becomes one of the main forces shaping the network.

Winter naturally shifts me south and toward lower elevations.

Spring gradually opens northern and higher-elevation locations.

Summer opens places such as South Dakota, Michigan, the Great Lakes region, and northern travel corridors.

Autumn begins opening southern routes again.

This means I can follow a broad climate envelope rather than a strict calendar.

Within that envelope, I retain enormous freedom.

The landscape changes while the underlying structure of my life remains remarkably consistent.

## My Nervous System Needs an Exit

Perhaps one of the most personally important realizations today is that my nervous system seems to need the **knowledge that I can leave**.

If I am somewhere and stop liking what is happening there, I need to know that I am not trapped.

With nomad life, if a campsite becomes noisy, uncomfortable, socially unpleasant, crowded, strange, or simply feels wrong to me, I can pack my camp during daylight and relocate.

I do not need to argue with anyone.

I do not need to explain why I am leaving.

The people who annoyed me do not even need to know that they were the reason.

There does not need to be drama for anybody.

I simply leave.

That is an enormous difference from fixed-location life.

Instead of asking:

**“How do I endure this environment?”**

I can ask:

**“Would another location suit me better?”**

That preserves my personal power without requiring me to control anyone else.

Their behavior remains theirs.

My location remains mine.

And every time I explore another campsite in the same general region, my knowledge of that region grows.

I gradually develop a personal field atlas of good places, backup places, quiet places, places with better cellular service, places with easy roads, places that work in certain weather, places to avoid on weekends, useful resupply locations, and routes between them.

The longer I travel, the stronger the system becomes.

## Novelty Without Chaos

I also realized that mobility satisfies another part of me.

I genuinely enjoy new locations.

My nervous system seems to want both:

**consistency and novelty.**

Thirteen days gives me enough time to settle, relax, establish routines, and become familiar with a place.

Then moving approximately 300 miles provides an entirely new landscape and new stimulation.

So my life can alternate between:

**settling → exploring → becoming comfortable → moving → discovering → settling again.**

This gives me novelty without requiring disorder.

The routine remains familiar.

The scenery changes.

## Relationships Without Dependency

I also thought quite a lot today about how relationships could fit naturally into this lifestyle.

If I meet a woman somewhere and there is mutual romantic or sexual interest, I may decide to remain in that general region longer, provided I can do so legally.

She might even invite me to stay at her home for a while.

But critically, my ability to function would never depend on continuing to stay there.

If she wanted space, I could simply return to a nearby legal camping location.

There would be no housing crisis.

No argument would be necessary.

She would not need to feel that asking for space threatened my survival.

And I would not need to feel trapped by somebody else's emotional state.

If the relationship paused and she later wanted to see me again, I could gradually route myself back toward that part of the country.

If the season did not make that region practical for nomad life, then she might simply need to wait until my seasonal route naturally returned there.

And if she wanted to see me sooner, she could travel to wherever I was currently camped. If she flew into a nearby airport, I could pick her up.

That creates the possibility of relationships based much more strongly on **mutual desire rather than logistical dependency**.

We spend time together because both of us want to.

We spend time apart when either person needs space.

Neither person's home becomes a cage for the other.

People could eventually become another kind of meaningful node in my travel network:

**someone I care about lives here.**

Then I can return when geography, season, and mutual interest align again.

## Nature as Religious Practice

Today I also developed clearer language for explaining my spirituality in relation to camping.

Visiting and camping in beautiful natural places is not merely recreation for me.

It is also a religious experience.

I consider nature and the land sacred.

My religion is the **Heathen Third Path**, a modern Norse Pagan path centered on nature, honor, hospitality, personal sovereignty, spiritual freedom, and respectful relationship with the land and other beings.

Camping therefore places me physically inside something I regard as sacred.

My portable technology also does not contradict this.

My religious path can integrate Norse Pagan spirituality with modern technology. Portable computers, antennas, solar power, communications technology, AI systems, and other advanced tools allow me to remain connected, creative, informed, and safe while spending extended periods close to nature.

The goal is not rejecting technology.

The goal is using technology without becoming separated from the natural world.

This feels increasingly like an important part of the Heathen Third Path itself.

## Ranger Communication and Privacy

Today I also worked out several short explanations that I can rehearse so conversations with rangers do not become stressful improvisation.

My normal explanation of my travel rhythm is essentially:

I stay around thirteen days and then usually travel roughly three hundred miles to another recreational camping area. Thirteen and three also have spiritual significance for me.

If asked where I am going next, I can simply explain that I keep my route flexible and usually decide closer to departure based on weather, access, and current conditions.

If asked whether the state associated with my documentation is where I am from, I can simply say that it is my home state and that I am traveling recreationally and camping temporarily.

I do not need to volunteer unnecessary details about how often I am physically present anywhere, my mail arrangements, my administrative structure, or other private matters that are irrelevant to camping compliance.

The important principle is:

**Be truthful.  
Be polite.  
Be compliant.  
Answer what is relevant.  
Do not unnecessarily surrender privacy.**

## My Nomad Intelligence Network

Another major piece of the system is technological.

I have begun adding every important campsite, travel node, and corridor location to a system where Gemini Spark can produce a daily morning nomad resource report.

That means my travel map is evolving into something more than a static collection of locations.

Each node can potentially report information such as:

- weather,
- fire restrictions,
- road conditions,
- land access,
- camping-rule changes,
- hunting-season concerns,
- closures,
- water availability,
- fuel and resupply conditions,
- cellular connectivity,
- library and resource availability,
- and other local factors.

Over time this could become a kind of **distributed nervous system for my nomad life**.

Instead of asking only, “Where can I go?”

The system can increasingly help answer:

**“Which available location is healthiest for me right now?”**

That is a much more powerful question.

## The Pattern Under Everything

Looking across everything that happened today, I see a surprisingly consistent pattern.

My ideal life is not based on controlling other people, controlling places, controlling nature, or forcing circumstances to remain unchanged.

It is based on **preserving my ability to respond**.

I cannot control every campsite.

I can choose another campsite.

I cannot control another person's emotions.

I can preserve enough independence that their emotions do not determine my survival.

I cannot control the weather.

I can migrate with the seasons.

I cannot control whether a location changes.

I can maintain a network of alternatives.

I cannot control which technical problem an AI system encounters.

I can build an architecture capable of routing around changing conditions.

Perhaps sovereignty is not absolute control.

Perhaps sovereignty is **retaining enough freedom, knowledge, tools, and alternatives that change does not easily take away one's center**.

That may be one of the clearest descriptions yet of what I have been trying to build.

## A Stable Life With Moving Coordinates

The phrase that keeps emerging from all of this is:

**Stable life. Flexible coordinates.**

My routines can remain stable.

My spirituality can remain stable.

My technology can remain stable.

My creative work can remain stable.

My budget can remain stable.

My exercise can remain stable.

My relationship with myself can remain stable.

The landscape does not have to remain stable.

In fact, allowing the landscape to change may be exactly what allows everything else to finally become calmer.

I do not need one perfect location forever.

I need a system that allows me to continually choose good locations for the conditions that exist now.

Maybe that is the deepest thing I understood today.

I have spent much of my life trying to make fixed places remain tolerable long enough for me to build stability inside them.

Perhaps I had the equation backward.

**I can build the stability inside myself and my systems, and let the places change.**

That feels like a major turning point.
