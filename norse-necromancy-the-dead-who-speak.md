## 1. How to read this document

This dossier gathers every major piece of evidence for **consulting, summoning, or magically raising the dead** in the Norse world: the poems, the sagas, the outside witnesses, the archaeology, the vocabulary, and the modern scholarship. It does not assume that "necromancy" was one thing. In the sources it is several things — prophecy from a völva, a son waking his mother at her mound, a god compelling a dead seeress to speak, a hanged man made to talk, and the violent revenant that must be expelled.

Two methodological rules govern the whole document:

**First, direct attestation is separated from inference.** Where the sources state a practice plainly, it is quoted. Where scholars infer (the *Völuspá* frame, *Hyndluljóð* as necromancy), the inference is labeled as such and the counter-evidence given. A section at the end (§11) grades every major claim by evidence strength.

**Second, everything here reaches us through Christian hands.** The Eddic poems were written down in 13th-century Christian Iceland; the sagas are 13th- and 14th-century literary products; the law codes that ban *útisetur* are Christian legislation. This does not make the material worthless — the same techniques appear in too many independent witnesses to be pure invention — but it means the *framing* (who is praised, who is shamed, what gets called heathenry) is often the author's, not the pagan's.

One more note on framing, stated plainly: **no source in this dossier treats consulting the dead as inherently immoral, as a violation of frith, or as spiritually corrupt in itself.** What the sources mark as shameful, dangerous, or extreme is located elsewhere — in the gendered stigma of male *seiðr*, in the peril of coercing unwilling dead, in the category-difference between a consulted ancestor and a hostile revenant. That distinction is one of the document's main findings, and it is grounded in the texts, not assumed.

Translations below are cited by translator. Old Norse is quoted where the wording carries weight, with translations alongside.

## 2. The vocabulary of death-magic

Old Norse has no single word that maps cleanly onto English "necromancy." What it has is a cluster of overlapping terms — techniques, practitioners, and social categories. Getting these right matters, because modern writing often blurs them into one sinister blob. The sources don't.

### *galdr* (pl. *galdrar*)

A sung or chanted incantation — a **technique**, not a moral category, and not inherently necromantic. *Galdrar* can heal, harm, protect, or bind; what makes one necromantic is its object (the dead), not its form. In *Ynglinga saga* ch. 7, Óðinn teaches "all these arts with runes and songs, which are called *galdrar*" (*allar þessar íþróttir kendi hann með rúnum ok ljóðum, þeim er galdrar heita*). The sung spell is the delivery system; the dead are one possible addressee.

### *seiðr*

A powerful fate-oriented magic of divination and manipulation — trance, spirit-work, the weaving and reading of wyrd. Its origins in the mythology are Vanir: *Ynglinga saga* ch. 4 states that Freyja "first taught *seiðr* among the Æsir, as was customary among the Vanir" (*kendi fyrst með Ásum seið, sem Vönum var títt*). **No source equates *seiðr* with necromancy.** The domains overlap in the figure of the völva, who may do both, but they are distinct concepts: *seiðr* is fate-magic; necromancy is dead-consultation. Conflating them is a modern habit the sources don't support.

### *spá*

Prophecy or foreknowledge as an activity; the practitioner is the *spákona* or *völva*. Scholarship generally treats *spá* as the more honorific term — a *spákona* is a prophetess, while *seiðkona* carries sorcerous overtones. The distinction is real but the boundary is porous; the same woman can be called both.

### *fjölkyngi*

A broad collective term — "much-knowing" — for magic/sorcery as a whole. Snorri uses it as a higher-order category under which arts like *seiðr* sit. Neil Price's *The Viking Way* treats *fjölkyngi* as Snorri's collective term for the magical arts. It is far too broad to mean necromancy.

### *útisetur* (also *úti-seta*, *sitja úti*)

Literally "sitting out" — sitting outdoors at night for the sake of sorcery or prophecy. Cleasby–Vigfusson's dictionary defines *úti-seta* as "a sitting out, in the open air, esp. of wizards sitting out at night for the sake of sorcery or prophesying." The Norwegian law codes explicitly associate it with waking otherworldly beings: *morð, fordæðu-skapi, ok spáfarar ok útisetu, **at vekja troll upp** ok fremja heiðni með því* — "murder, curse-making, prophecy-seeking and sitting-out, **to wake trolls up** and promote heathendom thereby" (Norges gamle Love i.19; cf. Gulathing law 137).

The classic literary instance is *Völuspá* 28: *Ein sat hon úti, þá er inn aldni kom* — "Alone she sat outside when the old one [= Óðinn] came" (see §3.2).

**Caution:** *útisetur* is a technique and a location, not a synonym for necromancy. It *can* involve the dead, but the claim that every sitting-out is corpse-consultation is not established. The law codes ban it as heathenry aimed at "waking trolls" — which tells us Christian legislators feared the technique, not that every practitioner was raising corpses.

### *valgaldr* — the closest match

The best lexical match for "necromancy" is **valgaldr**, "corpse-charms." Cleasby–Vigfusson glosses it as "charms, a kind of necromancy ascribed to Odin," and glosses *nás orð* ("corpse-words") as "necromancy" (cited in Stephen Mitchell's Harvard CHS chapter on *Hávamál* 157). In *Baldrs draumar* st. 4, Óðinn "spoke mighty *valgaldr*" (*nam hann vittugri valgaldr kveða*) to raise the dead völva. When the poem itself names the art, this is the word it uses.

### The mapping

| What we mean by "necromancy" | Old Norse term | Fit |
|---|---|---|
| Corpse-raising charm | *valgaldr* | **Strong** — direct lexical and literary match |
| Night sitting-out for prophecy/sorcery | *útisetur* | **Partial** — a technique, sometimes necromantic |
| Sung spell (any purpose) | *galdr* | Weak — form, not function |
| Magic in general | *fjölkyngi* | Weak — hypernym |
| Fate-magic | *seiðr* | Partial — overlapping personnel, distinct concept |
| Prophecy | *spá* | Weak — no corpse element |

## 3. Eddic evidence

The Poetic Edda contains the oldest literary witnesses to Norse death-magic. Three are unambiguous. Two famous candidates need correction.

### 3.1 *Baldrs draumar* — Óðinn wakes the dead völva

This is the clearest necromantic scene in the Edda, and the poem's own vocabulary names the art. The gods are troubled by Baldr's ominous dreams. Óðinn rides down into the underworld to learn what they mean:

> Then Othin rode | to the eastern door,
> There, he knew well, | was the wise-woman's grave;
> Magic he spoke | and mighty charms,
> Till spell-bound she rose, | and in death she spoke.
> — *Baldrs draumar* st. 4 (Bellows 1923)

The Old Norse of the third line is *nam hann vittugri **valgaldr** kveða* — he spoke mighty *valgaldr*, the corpse-charms. The dead woman's first words are a complaint:

> "What is the man, | to me unknown,
> That has made me travel | the troublous road?
> I was snowed on with snow, | and smitten with rain,
> And drenched with dew; | long was I dead."
> — st. 5

Óðinn, disguised as "Vegtam, son of Valtam" ("Way-accustomed, son of Battle-accustomed"), interrogates her about Baldr's fate. She answers — Höðr will be Baldr's bane, Váli will avenge him — and after each answer she tries to end the session:

> "Unwilling I spake, | and now would be still."
> — refrain, sts. 7, 9, 11

The key word is *nauðig* in the Old Norse: she rises **unwillingly, under compulsion**. Óðinn must keep her talking with repeated questions ("Wise-woman, cease not! I seek from thee / All to know that I fain would ask"). Finally she sees through his disguise — "Vegtam thou art not, as erstwhile I thought; / Othin thou art, the enchanter old" (st. 13) — and dismisses him: "Home ride, Othin, be ever proud; / For no one of men shall seek me more" (st. 14).

What this scene establishes:

- **The art has a name** (*valgaldr*), and the poem uses it at the moment of performance.
- **It works.** The dead woman genuinely knows the future — her prophecies are the poem's substance.
- **It is coercive here.** She rises unwillingly and resists. This is not communion; it is compelled testimony.
- **Óðinn does it himself.** The chief god is the tradition's master necromancer — not a marginal sorcerer but the All-Father, and the poem treats his knowledge-hunger as his defining trait rather than a crime.
- **Nothing in the poem condemns the act morally.** The völva's resistance is about her own unwillingness, not about the art being wicked. No frith is broken; no god objects.

Bellows' introductory note adds that *Völuspá* may likewise imply a völva risen from her grave — the frame is never narrated there, but the possibility was visible to the poem's first modern editor too.

### 3.2 *Völuspá* — the völva's speech and its necromantic frame

*Völuspá* ("The Prophecy of the Seeress") never narrates Óðinn raising the völva. What it gives us is the *speech itself*: a dead or otherworldly seeress recounting the world's beginning, its doom, and its rebirth, answering Óðinn's questions with the recurring refrain *vitoð ér enn, eða hvat?* — "would you know more, and what?"

Stephen Mitchell and others have argued the poem's frame is necromantic — that the völva speaks *from beyond death*, summoned by Óðinn (the *Valföðr*, "Father of the Slain," whom she addresses in st. 1: *viltu at ek, Valfǫðr, vel fyr telja forn spjǫll fira* — "would you have me, Valföðr, tell well the ancient tales of men"). This is an **inference**, not a narrated event, and it is debated in scholarship. What is directly in the text:

- St. 22 names Heiðr, the *seiðr*-practicing seeress ("Heiðr they called her... she knew *seiðr*"), linking the prophetic office to the magical art.
- St. 28 contains the *útisetur* motif: *Ein sat hon úti, þá er inn aldni kom* — in Bellows: "Alone I sat | when the Old One sought me, / The terror of gods, | and gazed in mine eyes." The seeress sits out alone at night; Óðinn comes to consult her.

The honest position: *Völuspá* attests the **consultation of a seeress by Óðinn** and preserves the *útisetur* image in st. 28, but the claim that she speaks as a raised corpse rests on scholarly inference from her addresses and the refrain — plausible, defended by Mitchell, but not stated in the poem. It is counted here as supporting evidence, not as a primary attestation.

### 3.3 *Grógaldr* — a mother woken at her mound

The counterweight to *Baldrs draumar*'s coercion. In *Grógaldr* ("Gróa's Spell," the first part of *Svipdagsmál*), the young hero Svipdagr goes to his dead mother's burial mound and calls her:

> 'Wake thee, Groa! | wake, mother good!
> At the doors of the dead | I call thee;
> Rememberest thou not | that thy son thou badest
> To the grave-mound | to come to thee?'
> — *Grógaldr* st. 1 (Master Works Edition)

Gróa answers — surprised, not compelled:

> 'What evil is it | that now hath befallen
> My son, the only one | born to me,
> That thou callest thy mother, | who is mouldering now,
> And hath left the world | of the living?'
> — st. 2

Crucially, **she had instructed him to do this**: "Rememberest thou not that thy son thou badest / To the grave-mound to come to thee?" This is pre-arranged, kin-sanctioned consultation. Svipdagr asks for what he needs — "Spells shalt thou sing me, and songs of protection, / mother, to guard thy son on his way" (st. 5) — and Gróa sings nine protective incantations over him: against hindrance, straying, drowning, execution, fetters, storms at sea, killing frost, night-travelers (including, in a late-Christian-era touch, "no evil witch shall work thee harm, / nor dead Christian woman shall do thee hurt," st. 13), and giants.

What *Grógaldr* establishes:

- **Consulting the dead can be consensual, protective, and maternal.** No coercion, no unwillingness, no shame.
- **The mound is the venue.** The dead are reached *at the grave-mound* — the boundary between worlds has a geography.
- **The knowledge sought is practical magic**: protective spells for a dangerous journey. Necromancy as information-gathering in its purest form.
- **Kinship legitimizes it.** The dead mother *wants* to help. The poem presents this as entirely proper.

Set beside *Baldrs draumar*, the two poems define the spectrum: at one end the compelled stranger-dead, at the other the willing kin-dead. Both work. Neither is condemned.

### 3.4 *Hávamál* 157 — the twelfth charm

In the *Ljóðatal* ("Song-list"), Óðinn catalogues his magical knowledge. The twelfth charm is explicitly necromantic:

> A twelfth I know, | if high on a tree
> I see a hanged man swing;
> So do I write | and color the runes
> That forth he fares,
> And to me talks.
> — *Hávamál* 157 (Bellows 1923)

**(Numbering note:** this is the **twelfth** charm in the catalogue, standing at stanza 157 in standard poem numbering. Some older references call it the "18th charm" — that is a different charm, at st. 163/164. The hanged-man charm is the twelfth.)

Mitchell observes that this stanza answers *Hávamál* 71's cynical proverb *nýtr manngi nás* — "a dead man is good for nothing." Óðinn's charm proves the opposite: a dead man, properly addressed with written and reddened runes, **talks** — and what the dead know is worth having. The stanza's placement in Óðinn's own boast-catalogue makes corpse-consultation part of the god's *wisdom*, not his wickedness. It is listed alongside charms for healing, battle, love, and safe voyaging — one tool among many.

Mitchell's full study ("Óðinn, Charms, and Necromancy: *Hávamál* 157 in its Nordic and European Contexts," Harvard Center for Hellenic Studies) argues the motif has deep northern roots rather than being a late classical borrowing: "these necromantic practices and belief systems had evolved in northern Europe over a very long time with ongoing reticulations between vernacular and learned belief systems."

### 3.5 *Hyndluljóð* — a necessary correction

*Hyndluljóð* ("The Song of Hyndla") opens with Freyja waking the giantess Hyndla:

> *Vaki, mær meyja! Vaki, mín vina, / Hyndla systir, er í helli býr!*
> "Wake up, girl of girls! Wake up, my friend, / sister Hyndla, who lives in a cave!"

Freyja needs Hyndla to recite the young Óttarr's genealogy — again, the dead-or-hidden past as a source of needed knowledge. But **Hyndla is a living giantess asleep in a cave**, not a corpse; *vaki* ("wake!") is used of sleep as it is everywhere else. The poem is a structural parallel to necromantic "waking" — the same verb, the same interrogation of a recumbent knower — but it is **not evidence of corpse consultation** and should not be cited as such. It belongs in the dossier only as a boundary marker: this is where the "waking" motif shades into ordinary waking.

### 3.6 *Helreið Brynhildar* — a necessary correction

After Brynhildr's death she is burned — two bale-fires, her body in a wagon — and rides the Hel-road, where she debates a giantess at a house by the way. Her final stanza:

> "Ever with grief and all too long / Are men and women born in the world; / But yet we shall live our lives together, / Sigurth and I. Sink down, Giantess!"
> — *Helreið Brynhildar* st. 14 (Bellows)

This is **postmortem speech during the Hel-journey**, not a living practitioner summoning her. No one performs *valgaldr*; no one sits at her mound to question her. It is relevant to the dossier's theme — the dead *speak*, with knowledge and will — but it is not ritual necromancy. Counting it as such would blur the very categories this document is trying to keep sharp.

### What the Edda establishes, taken together

Three poems attest the practice directly (*Baldrs draumar*, *Grógaldr*, *Hávamál* 157); one supplies the consultation frame and the *útisetur* image (*Völuspá*); two famous candidates do not survive scrutiny as necromancy (*Hyndluljóð*, *Helreið Brynhildar*). Across the genuine attestations the pattern is consistent: **the dead know things the living need, the techniques to reach them are named arts (*valgaldr*, rune-charm, mound-calling), and the poems treat the art as effective — with the moral coloring supplied by the relationship (coerced stranger vs. willing kin), never by the art itself.**

## 4. Saga evidence

The sagas move the motif from myth into something closer to social reality — or at least into the 13th-century imagination of it. Here the dead are not only oracles but neighbors: dangerous ones to be managed, kin to be consulted, corpses to be compelled.

### 4.1 *Hervarar saga ok Heiðreks* — the Waking of Angantýr

The most famous saga necromancy. Hervör, daughter of the berserk Angantýr, travels to Sámsey — where her father and his eleven brothers lie buried in mounds — to claim the cursed sword Tyrfingr. She crosses the grave-fires at night and calls the dead:

> "Wake up, Angantýr, / Hervor awakes you, the only daughter born to you and Sváfa; give me from your grave your sharp-edged blade, the one the dwarves made for Sigrlami."
> — *Hervarar saga*, "The Waking of Angantýr," st. 1 (trans. Crawford 2017, via published excerpts)

She wakes them all: "Hervarð, Hjorvarð, / Hrani, Angantýr! / I awake you all under the tree roots…" (st. 2), and when they resist she threatens them with discomfort in the grave unless they yield the sword (st. 4). Angantýr answers from the mound:

> "You've gone crazy… when you dare to wake up dead men."
> — st. 5 (trans. Crawford 2017, via published excerpts)

He yields Tyrfingr in the end — the magic works — but the framing is the dossier's most extreme: a lone woman crossing grave-fires at night, threatening corpses, called mad for daring it. Note the precision of the social marking: Hervör is not called *evil* or *oath-breaking*. She is called **crazy** — the act is insane in its daring, not wicked in its morals. The sword she wins is itself cursed, which colors the episode, but the curse is on Tyrfingr, not on waking the dead.

**Sourcing note:** these stanzas are quoted from excerpts of the Crawford 2017 translation as indexed in published sources; the authorized scholarly edition is Christopher Tolkien's *Saga Heiðreks konungs ins vitra* (1960). Readers wanting the full sequence should consult Tolkien.

### 4.2 *Eyrbyggja saga* — draugar, Þórgunna, and the door-doom

*Eyrbyggja saga* is the richest saga source on the active dead — and the most instructive about social attitudes, because it shows a community *managing* the dead with law, ritual, and eventually Christian rite.

**Þórólfr halt-foot (bægifótr), the hostile revenant.** After his death Þórólfr "lay not quiet." The saga piles up the classic draugr signs: oxen yoked to his corpse-cart go mad; cattle that come near his mound bellow till they die; birds that settle on his howe fall dead; the herdsman is found "all coal-blue, and every bone in him was broken"; Þórólfr walks home in winter and troubles the household until his widow dies of it; then "Thorolf took to walking so wide through the dale that he laid waste all steads therein, and so great was the trouble from his walking that he slew some men, and some fled away; but all those who died were seen in his company" (chs. 31–34, Morris & Magnússon 1892). He is eventually dug up, found monstrous, and burned (ch. 63).

Þórólfr is the **anti-ancestor**: the dead as contagion and predator. He is not consulted; he is contained. His category is *draugr*/*aptrgangr* ("one who walks again"), and the saga's treatment of him is the strongest evidence that the Norse distinguished sharply between the honored dead and the dangerous dead.

**Þórgunna, the purposeful dead.** A Hebridean woman who settles at Fróðá, Þórgunna leaves precise burial instructions: her bed-gear is to be burned, her wealth given to the church, her body carried to Skálholt (chs. 50–51). When the household disobeys — keeping the rich bed-hangings — she returns. But her return is not Þórólfr's: she comes back to *prepare food*, working purposefully and non-violently in the household, and her retained bedding becomes the focus of the Fróðá hauntings. The dead here have **intentions that outlive them**, and violating a dead person's instructions has consequences — a belief about the will of the dead, not about necromancy, but part of the same worldview: the dead are still agents.

**The door-doom at Fróðá (ch. 55) — the community sues the ghosts.** When the hauntings escalate — the drowned Þóroddr and his men dripping at the fire, Þórir viðleggr ("Wooden-leg") and his moldy company casting filth, eighteen dead and the household down to seven — Kjartan seeks counsel from Snorri the Priest. The remedy is extraordinary:

> So Snorri sent the priest out to Frodis-water with Kiartan… Thereto he added the counsel to burn Thorgunna's bed-gear, and **summon all those who walked, to a door-doom**; and he bade the priest sing the hours there, and hallow water and shrive all folk.

Kjartan burns the bed-gear, then formally summons the revenants: "Kiartan summoned Thorir Woodenleg, and Thord Kausi summoned goodman Thorod, in that they went about that household **without leave, and despoiled men both of life and luck**; all were summoned who sat by the fires. Then was a door-doom named, and these cases put forward; and **it was done in all matters even as at a doom of the Thing: verdicts were delivered, cases summed up, and doom given.**"

As each sentence is pronounced, the ghosts leave — Þórir: "Here have I sat while sit I might"; the shepherd: "Go I now hencefrom"; Þórgríma Witch-face: "Here while abiding was meet I abode"; Þóroddr last: "Meseems little peace is here; so get us all gone otherwhere." The priest then hallows the house, and the hauntings end.

The door-doom is a remarkable datum for this dossier's central question. The community treats the walking dead as **legal persons subject to frith**: they are summoned, charged ("without leave... despoiled men both of life and luck"), tried, and sentenced — and the sentences *work*. The dead are inside the law, not outside it. Expulsion is a legal act as much as a ritual one. (The Christian priest's rites layered over the proceeding are the 13th-century author's toolkit; the legal form is the older stratum.)

### 4.3 *Grettis saga* — Glamr

Glámr is a big Swedish shepherd, hired despite warnings, who fears nothing — "I am not afraid of ghosts. It will be the less dull." He confronts the haunting at Þórhallsstaðir and is killed by it; he returns as a revenant of exceptional violence. Grettir fights him through the night, kills and beheads him, but with his dying breath Glámr lays a curse on Grettir:

> "I also lay this upon you that these eyes of mine will be always before your sight, and you will find it hard to be alone and this will bring you to your death."

The curse takes hold. Grettir — the strongest man in Iceland — becomes "so frightened of the dark that he dared not go anywhere alone at night. Apparitions of every kind came before him." The saga notes it passed into language: men speak of **"Glam's eyes" or "Glam visions"** when things appear otherwise than as they are (chs. 32–35; cf. Fox & Pálsson 1974).

Glámr belongs with Þórólfr: the draugr as **pollution and psychological wound**, not oracle. His curse is itself a kind of death-magic — the dead man's words reshaping the living man's fate — but it is hostile magic, the mirror image of Gróa's protective songs. The pair, Gróa and Glámr, bracket the moral range of the speaking dead: the mother's blessing, the revenant's curse. Neither is "necromancy" as a rite; both show the culture's premise that **the dead speak with power**.

### 4.4 *Ynglinga saga* — Óðinn, *seiðr*, and *ergi*

Snorri's *Ynglinga saga* (chs. 4 and 7) gives the fullest prose account of Óðinn's magical arts, in Old Norse text that survives to be read directly:

- **Ch. 4:** Freyja "first taught *seiðr* among the Æsir, as was customary among the Vanir" (*kendi fyrst með Ásum seið, sem Vönum var títt*). The art's mythic origin is Vanir and female. Óðinn also preserves and enchants the severed head of Mímir so that it speaks and reveals hidden things — **an oracle made from a dead head**, kept by the god as a counselor.
- **Ch. 7:** "Sometimes he woke dead men up out of the earth, or sat beneath the hanged; for that reason he was called lord of the *draugar* or lord of the hanged" (*En stundum vakti hann upp dauða menn or jörðu, eða settist undir hanga; fyrir því var hann kallaðr drauga dróttinn eða hanga dróttinn*). "All these arts he taught with runes and songs, which are called *galdrar*." And of *seiðr*: "such *ergi* accompanies this *fjölkyngi* that men deemed it shameful to practice, and it was taught to priestesses" (*En þessi fjölkyngi... fylgir svá mikil ergi, at eigi þótti karlmönnum skamlaust við at fara, ok var gyðjunum kend sú íþrótt*).

Three things matter here for the dossier:

1. **Óðinn's necromancy is stated as fact in the mythic history.** Waking the dead and sitting beneath the hanged are among the arts that make him Óðinn — listed alongside rune-magic and prophecy, not set apart as his dark secret.
2. **The shame attaches to *seiðr*, and it attaches to *men*.** *Ergi* — the complex of unmanliness, effeminacy, sexual passivity as the culture constructed it — is what makes *seiðr* shameful *for men*. It was "taught to priestesses" instead. This is a **gendered** stigma, not a stigma on death-magic as such: nothing in the chapter says waking the dead is shameful; what is shameful is a man doing *seiðr*. The distinction is critical and often lost.
3. **Mímir's head** is the mythic prototype of the consulted dead as counselor — the wise dead kept close, speaking secrets. It rhymes with Gróa and with the *Baldrs draumar* völva: the dead as intelligence assets.

(Snorri's euhemerism — Óðinn as a human sorcerer-king from the east — is the Christian author's frame; see §8. What matters is that even the euhemerized account **preserves the necromantic details**.)

### 4.5 *útisetur* in saga and law — a caution

The Norwegian law codes ban *spáfarar ok útisetur* — prophecy-seeking and sitting-out — "to wake trolls up" (*at vekja troll upp*). *Þorsteins þáttr uxafóts* is sometimes cited for a literary *útisetur* scene (ÍF 13, pp. 341–70; trans. George Clark in *The Complete Sagas of Icelanders* vol. 4), but the scene's contents could not be verified against the text in this research pass. **Do not assert the þáttr as an *útisetur* instance without consulting the Clark translation or ÍF edition.** The law-code evidence stands on its own: Christian legislators believed sitting-out was aimed at waking otherworldly beings, which is evidence for the *fear* of the technique, not a description of it.

### What the sagas establish, taken together

The sagas attest three distinct dead: the **consulted dead** (Mímir's head; the implied licit background of Gróa's rite), the **compelled dead** (Hervör's mound-waking — effective, daring, called mad but not wicked), and the **hostile dead** (Þórólfr, Glámr — to be burned, beheaded, or sued out of the house). The door-doom shows the dead inside the legal order. The *ergi* complex shows stigma attaching to gender-transgression in *seiðr*, not to death-consultation itself.

## 5. External witnesses — Ibn Fadlan and Tacitus

Two outside observers, eight centuries apart, describe Germanic peoples' dealings with seeresses and the dead. Neither is a transparent window, but both corroborate the literary picture from outside the tradition.

### Ibn Fadlan's *Risāla* (10th century)

The Arab traveler Aḥmad ibn Faḍlān witnessed a Rus' chieftain's ship funeral on the Volga (c. 921–922). The Rus' were Scandinavian-connected though culturally mixed, so this is adjacent evidence, not a direct record of Norse practice. The relevant details:

- A slave girl volunteers to die with her master. Lifted three times over a wooden frame like a door, she reports visions: first her father and mother, then all her departed kin, then her master "in a beautiful green paradise," waiting for her.
- An old woman called the "Angel of Death" (*malak al-mawt*) presides — "a gloomy, corpulent woman, neither young nor old" — and it is she who kills the slave girls.
- The girl is then strangled and stabbed, and burned with the chieftain in the ship.

**Framing:** this is **visionary access to the dead and the afterlife at a funeral**, mediated by ritual specialists — not a necromancer raising the dead to question them. The slave girl's visions parallel the Eddic premise that the boundary is permeable and the dead can be *seen and spoken with* under ritual conditions. Peter Schjødt's analysis notes the ritual ambiguity and apotropaic fear of the dead surrounding the funeral. It belongs in the dossier as evidence for the **permeability premise**, not as necromancy proper.

### Tacitus, *Germania* 8 (1st century CE)

Eight centuries earlier, Tacitus reports that the Germanic peoples "believe that there resides in women an element of holiness and a gift of prophecy; and so they do not scorn to ask their advice, or lightly disregard their replies." He names Veleda — honored by many as divine — and notes earlier prophetesses like Aurinia received similar reverence.

**Framing:** this proves the **high social status of female seers** deep in the Germanic past, not necromancy. But it is the deep root of the völva's honored position in §7.1: the Norse völva stands in a tradition of revered prophetesses a thousand years old by the saga age. (Caesar's *Gallic War* 1.50, on Germanic matrons fixing the auspicious battle day by lots, is the standard comparative parallel.)

## 6. Archaeology — mounds, reopening, and the active dead

Archaeology cannot show us a *valgaldr* being spoken. What it can show is how the living handled the dead — and the handling implies the beliefs.

### *Haugbrot* — mound-breaking

Many Viking Age burial mounds were reopened — *haugbrot* — and the reopenings were often not simple looting. Research on the phenomenon (e.g., "Haugbrott og herskermakt: om gravrøveri som ritual") finds that reopeners knew mound construction, likely knew who was buried there and what had been deposited; removing hundreds of cubic meters of material could not be done secretly, so local elites or the community likely consented; and removing symbolically valuable objects could legitimate continuity or transfer of rulership.

The Grønhaug ship burial, for example, was reopened in the later 10th century. Birch bark once theorized as ritual flooring "to avoid disturbing beings/ghosts in the mound" is now considered uncertain by the excavators.

**The explicit scholarly warning:** explanations centered on skeletal remains — including necromancy — must be placed in a broad social and ritual context. Missing bones do not necessarily prove deliberate removal, and reopening does not prove corpse-consultation. *Haugbrot* is evidence that the dead remained **socially active property** — their mounds could be entered, their goods redistributed, their presence negotiated — which is the material correlate of the literary premise that the dead are still agents. It is not evidence of necromantic rites.

### Bent and "killed" weapons

Ritually bent or destroyed weapons are a well-known Iron Age funerary phenomenon — in cremation burials and weapon deposits. Competing explanations: decommissioning grave goods, sacrifice to gods, apotropaic measures against the dead's return. **No single explanation is established**, and this research pass secured no verified academic citation settling the question. It is mentioned here only to mark the boundary: do not cite bent swords as necromancy evidence.

### Bog bodies

Mostly pre-Viking-Age (Iron Age) Danish and North German; no demonstrated link to necromantic practice. Mentioned only to exclude.

### Gardeła and Price — the archaeology of magic and death

- **Leszek Gardeła** ("(Magic) Staffs in the Viking Age," 2016; "Magic, Women and Death in the Viking Age," 2019; ed., *The Norse Sorceress: Mind and Materiality in the Viking World*, Oxbow 2023): staffs and amulets mark ritual specialists in graves (e.g., Fyrkat); his core archaeological claim is **ambivalence** — magic-workers could be buried with honor *or* stoned and executed ("Buried with Honour and Stoned to Death? The Ambivalence of Viking Age Magic in the Light of Archaeology," 2009). The material record mirrors the literary spectrum: the practitioner is significant, and significance cuts both ways.
- **Neil Price** (*The Viking Way: Magic and Mind in Late Iron Age Scandinavia*, 2nd ed., Oxbow 2019; *Children of Ash and Elm*, 2020): magic as a major domain of **women's power**, linking them with gods, the dead, and the future; sorcery and warfare intertwined. Price's framing supports the dossier's central distinction: the völva's traffic with the unseen — including the dead — was a recognized source of female authority, not a marginal deviance.

### What archaeology establishes

The dead were **handled as ongoing social persons**: mounds entered and renegotiated, grave goods decommissioned or redistributed, ritual specialists buried with the tools of their office. Nothing excavated proves a necromantic rite; everything excavated is consistent with a worldview in which the dead remain present, potent, and worth managing — the worldview the poems and sagas articulate.

## 7. How Norse society held it — the spectrum of attitudes

This is the dossier's central question, and the sources answer it with more subtlety than either "revered" or "reviled." Four findings, each grounded in the texts.

### 7.1 The völva: honored and feared

The fullest portrait of a working seeress is Þorbjörg lítilvölva ("Little Völva") in *Eiríks saga rauða* ch. 4. During a famine in Greenland she is summoned to prophesy, and the reception is that of an honored guest of high status:

- She is escorted respectfully and given the **high seat**, with a cushion stuffed with hen-feathers.
- Her costume is a catalog of office: a blue cloak set with stones, glass beads around her neck, a lambskin hood lined with white catskin, catskin gloves, fur shoes, a staff topped with brass and set with stones, a pouch of magical tools, an ivory-handled knife.
- She is fed porridge of kid's milk and the hearts of every animal at hand.
- The farmer's wife Guðríð — a Christian, reluctant — is prevailed upon to sing the *varðlokur* ("ward-songs," the spirit-calling songs); as she sings, the spirits grow attentive, and Þorbjörg declares the *spá* will now succeed.
- She predicts the famine's end and Guðríð's personal future — marriage, a distinguished lineage.

She is **feasted, seated high, and consulted** — and simultaneously a religiously contested figure in a Christian-authored narrative (Guðríð's reluctance is the author's signal). The völva is not marginal; she is *liminal* — brought in from outside at need, treated with expensive honor, and slightly dangerous to have around. Tacitus' Veleda, honored as divine eight centuries earlier (§5), is the deep root of this status: the Germanic prophetess was already a figure of reverence before the Viking Age began.

**What this means for necromancy:** the völva's office includes traffic with the unseen — spirits, the dead, the future — and the office itself is honored. When the practitioner is a woman doing *spá*, the culture's response is hospitality and payment, not persecution.

### 7.2 Male *seiðr* and *ergi* — where the shame actually lands

The tradition's sharpest stigma falls on **men who practice *seiðr***, and it is a gendered stigma, not a necromantic one. Two witnesses:

- *Lokasenna* 24: Loki taunts Óðinn — "they said you practiced *seiðr* on Sámsey, and beat on a lid like the *vǫlur*; in a witch's guise you went through a nation of men, and I thought that was *argr*'s way" (*ok hugða ek þat args aðal*). Bellows: "wovest spells like a witch... were these womanish ways, I ween."
- *Ynglinga saga* ch. 7: "such *ergi* accompanies this *fjölkyngi* that men deemed it shameful to practice, and it was taught to priestesses."

*Ergi* (adj. *argr*) is the Norse concept-cluster of unmanliness — effeminacy, sexual passivity, cowardice as the culture constructed them. The shame is in **a man doing women's magic**, not in the magic's object. Nothing in either passage says waking the dead is shameful; *Baldrs draumar* and *Hávamál* 157 show Óðinn doing exactly that as an expression of his wisdom-hunger, and no one in those poems calls it *ergi*. (Loki's taunt is about *seiðr* performed in a witch's guise — the gender-crossing is the charge.)

The common modern compression — "*seiðr* was necromancy and it was shameful" — is wrong on both halves. *Seiðr* is fate-magic, not necromancy (§2), and its shame is gendered. **No source in this dossier stigmatizes consulting the dead as such.**

### 7.3 The draugr is not the ancestor

The tradition maintains a categorical distinction the modern reader must not collapse:

- **The consulted dead** — Gróa at her mound, Mímir's speaking head, the *Baldrs draumar* völva — are *sources*. They are approached deliberately, at the mound or grave, for knowledge or aid. Gróa was invited; Mímir is kept as a counselor; even the compelled völva is an informant, not a predator.
- **The hostile dead** — Þórólfr halt-foot, Glámr, the Fróðá walkers — are *threats*. They are not consulted; they are burned, beheaded, or sued. Their defining trait is that they act **without leave** — the door-doom charges them with going "about that household without leave, and despoil[ing] men both of life and luck."

The honored background is ancestor veneration: the *dísablót* (sacrifice to the *dísir*, the female ancestral powers) and the *álfablót* (sacrifice to the *álfar*, with ancestral associations) belong to the ritual calendar. The *dísir* are approached with offerings; the draugr is met with fire and law. **One does not "necromance" a draugr; one expels it. One does not expel Gróa; one wakes her and listens.**

This is why the door-doom matters so much: it shows the dead as **subjects of frith**. They can violate it (haunting "without leave") and be sentenced for it. The legal order *includes* the dead — which means the dead were not outside society but a special class within it, with rights, obligations, and procedures.

### 7.4 Effective but costly — the dominant literary pattern

Across the dossier, necromancy is never routine and never simply forbidden. The pattern is: **it works, and it costs.**

- Óðinn's *valgaldr* works — and the völva rises *nauðig*, unwilling, and dismisses him forever.
- Hervör's waking works — and she is called mad for daring it, and the sword she wins is cursed.
- Gróa's rite works — and it is the gentle exception that proves the rule: willing kin, pre-arranged, protective.
- The twelfth charm works — and it belongs to Óðinn's secret catalogue, the hard-won wisdom of the god who gave an eye for knowledge.

Cost here means **danger, daring, and spiritual weight** — not moral condemnation. The sources treat compelling the dead the way they treat handling a drawn sword: potent, legitimate in the right hands for the right reasons, and not to be done lightly. That is a far cry from the Christian-medieval framing of necromancy as diabolism, and the dossier should not import that framing.

## 8. The Christian filter

Every source in this dossier passed through Christian hands, and the filter leaves fingerprints:

- **Law codes criminalize the technique.** The Norwegian laws ban *útisetur* and *spáfarar* as heathenry aimed at "waking trolls" (*at vekja troll upp*). This is legislation *against* a practice the legislators believed was real and ongoing — evidence the sitting-out tradition survived into the Christian era, framed now as crime.
- **Snorri euhemerizes Óðinn.** In *Ynglinga saga* Óðinn is a human sorcerer-king from the east whose "magic" is explained naturalistically. Yet even the euhemerized account **preserves the necromantic details** — waking the dead, sitting under the hanged, Mímir's speaking head. The technique survived the demotion of the god.
- **The Fróðá door-doom is resolved by a Christian priest's rites.** The legal proceeding against the ghosts is the older stratum; the hallowing with holy water and the singing of the hours is the 13th-century author's toolkit for neutralizing the pagan dead. The saga needs *both* law and liturgy to lay the haunting — a telling hybrid.
- **Demonization is later and thinner than often assumed.** The sources collected here — even Christian-authored ones — do not present Gróa's mound-rite or the völva's *spá* as diabolical. The moral panic around necromancy as devil-worship belongs to the later medieval learned tradition, not to the Norse material itself.

The filter distorts framing more than fact. What it cannot do is invent *valgaldr*, the door-doom, or the twelfth charm across independent witnesses.

## 9. What the scholars say

- **Stephen A. Mitchell** ("Óðinn, Charms, and Necromancy," Harvard Center for Hellenic Studies; *Witchcraft and Magic in the Nordic Middle Ages*, 2011): the Nordic necromantic material is not a late classical or Christian borrowing — "these necromantic practices and belief systems had evolved in northern Europe over a very long time with ongoing reticulations between vernacular and learned belief systems." *Hávamál* 157 refutes the proverb that a dead man is good for nothing.
- **Neil Price** (*The Viking Way: Magic and Mind in Late Iron Age Scandinavia*, 2nd ed., Oxbow 2019): magic as a major domain of **women's power**, linking them with gods, the dead, and the future; sorcery and warfare intertwined; *fjölkyngi* as Snorri's collective category. The völva's authority flows from her traffic with the unseen — the dead included.
- **Catharina Raudvere** ("Trolldómr in Early Medieval Scandinavia," in Ankarloo & Clark, eds., *Witchcraft and Magic in Europe: The Middle Ages*, Univ. of Pennsylvania Press, 2001): gender- and power-centered analysis; discusses Gullveig/Heiðr (*Völuspá* 21–22) and Freyja teaching *seiðr* to the Æsir; critical of the uncritical "shamanism" paradigm.
- **Leszek Gardeła** (*(Magic) Staffs in the Viking Age*, 2016; *Magic, Women and Death in the Viking Age*, 2019; ed., *The Norse Sorceress*, Oxbow 2023): the archaeology of ritual specialists; **ambivalence** as the core finding — magic-workers buried with honor *or* stoned and executed. The material record mirrors the literary spectrum.
- **Clive Tolley** (*Shamanism in Norse Myth and Magic*, FF Communications 296–297, Helsinki, 2009): the most exhaustive source-critical study of *seiðr*; skeptical of loose "shamanism" labels; analyzes *seiðr* as a complex of divinatory and aggressive techniques. (Specific page-level claims not re-verified in this pass.)
- **John Lindow** (*Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs*, Oxford, 2001): the standard mythological reference; treats the dead and draugar as established mythic categories. (Specific claims not re-verified in this pass.)

The scholarly consensus, so far as one exists: **there was a genuine northern tradition of consulting and compelling the dead, distinct from *seiðr* proper, practiced within a social order that honored its female practitioners, gender-shamed its male ones, legally managed its dangerous dead, and preserved its techniques even through Christian retelling.**

## 10. Synthesis — was consulting the dead normal or transgressive?

The evidence supports a conditional answer, not a binary one. Six propositions, each grounded above:

1. **Divination by female seers was normal, public, and honored.** Þorbjörg lítilvölva is feasted, high-seated, and consulted; Tacitus attests the reverence eight centuries deep. The völva's traffic with the unseen is her office, not her deviance.

2. **Consulting willing kin-dead was intelligible and licit.** Gróa *invites* her son's call at the mound and sings protection over him. The poem presents this as proper — a mother's aid, sought at the grave-mound, in the proper form.

3. **Coercive corpse-raising was effective but marked as extreme.** Óðinn must compel the unwilling völva (*nauðig reis*); Hervör is called mad for daring to wake dead men; the hanged-man charm sits in Óðinn's hard-won secret catalogue. The marking is about **daring and cost**, not wickedness — no source calls it evil, oath-breaking, or frith-violating.

4. **Male participation in *seiðr*-adjacent magic carried gendered shame (*ergi*)** — while female practitioners were stigmatized differently (as dangerous, not unmanly). The shame attaches to gender-transgression, not to death-consultation. Óðinn wakes the dead as an expression of wisdom, and the poems do not blush for him.

5. **The draugr is not the ancestor.** The revenant is a threat to be expelled — by burning, beheading, or lawsuit — categorically distinct from the consulted dead. Confusing the two is the most common modern error about this material.

6. **All of it reaches us through Christian filters** — law codes criminalize *útisetur*; saga priests exorcise hauntings; Snorri euhemerizes Óðinn — yet the techniques (*valgaldr*, sitting under the hanged, mound-waking, the door-doom) survive in enough independent witnesses that Mitchell's conclusion stands: a deep-rooted northern tradition of necromantic practice, **neither everyday routine nor mere literary fantasy**.

The lore idea that "society" holds about it, then, is not one idea but a **spectrum with a clear structure**: honor for the völva, legitimacy for kin-consultation, awe and wariness for the compelled dead, legal process for the hostile dead, gendered shame for men who cross into *seiðr*. At no point does the tradition itself declare the consultation of the dead immoral. The dead speak; the wise listen; the reckless compel; the community manages the rest.

## 13. Source notes — dating and manuscripts

- **Poetic Edda poems** (*Baldrs draumar*, *Völuspá*, *Hávamál*, *Grógaldr*, *Hyndluljóð*, *Helreið Brynhildar*, *Lokasenna*): preserved principally in the **Codex Regius** (GKS 2365 4to, c. 1270s, Iceland); *Baldrs draumar* survives only in the Arnamagnæan manuscript AM 748 I 4to. Composition dates are debated — most scholars place the mythological poems between the 9th and 12th centuries, with *Völuspá* often dated c. 1000. All are pre-Christian or transitional *material* in 13th-century *manuscripts*.
- **Snorri's *Edda*** (Prose Edda): c. 1220s, Christian Iceland.
- ***Ynglinga saga***: first saga of Snorri's *Heimskringla*, c. 1230s.
- ***Eyrbyggja saga***: c. 1230–1260.
- ***Grettis saga***: c. 1310–1320.
- ***Eiríks saga rauða***: 13th century (Þorbjörg's *spá* in ch. 4).
- ***Hervarar saga***: 13th century (legendary saga; the "Waking of Angantýr" verses may preserve older material).
- **Norwegian law codes** (Gulathing, NGL i.19): 12th–13th century Christian legislation, preserving bans on heathen practices.
- **Ibn Fadlan**: 921–922 CE, contemporary with the Viking Age.
- **Tacitus**: 98 CE — eight centuries before the Viking Age; deep background, not direct evidence.

**Final methodological note.** This dossier treats the Eddic poems as witnesses to *belief and motif*, the sagas as witnesses to *13th-century imagination of the pagan past*, the laws as witnesses to *Christian anxiety about surviving practice*, and archaeology as witness to *handling of the dead*. None of these is a field recording of a *valgaldr*.
